US fires full 24-round load in third successful test of reusable rocket system. This article comes from Interesting Engineering, written by Aamir Khollam, and was published on November 23rd, 2026.

Lockheed Martin has put a new rocket training system through its third successful flight test, moving closer to a potential replacement for the practice rockets the U.S. Army uses today. The November demonstration took place at Yuma Proving Ground in Arizona, and it involved firing twenty-four training rounds from a High Mobility Artillery Rocket System, better known as HIMARS.

The system is called the Joint Reduced Range Rocket, or J-R-3. It is a training system, designed to make live-fire exercises more affordable. Its reusable launch hardware could help the Army conduct more frequent rocket drills without relying on expensive operational ammunition.

The idea behind J-R-3 is straightforward, and it speaks to a problem rocket artillery units deal with all the time. Crews need regular live-fire exercises to maintain proficiency. Training with operational ammunition, though, can place considerable pressure on military budgets. Range availability and the cost of that ammunition also limit how frequently crews can practice. In other words, the Army wants its launcher crews to train realistically and often, but the tools it uses for that training are expensive, and the places where those exercises can be held are not always easy to come by.

Lockheed Martin is developing J-R-3 around established rocket motor technology. That choice is meant to help the company avoid the expense of building an entirely new propulsion system from scratch. The design focuses on reducing the cost of repeated training exercises while retaining realistic firing characteristics, so the practice still resembles the real thing. The program is aimed at U.S. Army requirements, and Lockheed Martin says it could eventually serve international military partners as well.

The November test examined the rocket’s range, its flight path, and its accuracy. The system also features an adaptable payload that produces visible marking effects. Those effects allow instructors to evaluate rocket placement during exercises. That is a practical detail, and it matters for training. If crews are going to learn from a live-fire drill, instructors need a way to see where the rounds went and judge how well they were placed.

This was not the first time Lockheed Martin had flown J-R-3. The company previously conducted another successful flight test in August. That demonstration also involved launching rockets from a HIMARS launcher. The November exercise expanded the testing with a complete twenty-four-round salvo. That full load is a meaningful step, because the system is built around a reusable twenty-four-round launch pod. Firing the complete set of training rounds is a broader demonstration than launching a smaller number of rockets, and it puts the hardware through the kind of volume the design is meant to support.

One of J-R-3’s most interesting engineering features is that launch infrastructure. The system pairs individual training rounds with the reusable twenty-four-round pod. The launch tubes themselves are reloadable, which allows crews to reuse critical launch hardware across multiple training cycles. Lockheed Martin’s argument is that this approach could lower the overall expense of maintaining regular live-fire programs. Instead of treating important pieces of the launch equipment as something used once and discarded, crews could reload the tubes and put the same pod back to work.

J-R-3 is designed to support both HIMARS and the M270 Multiple Launch Rocket System. Those platforms give the Army flexibility when organizing artillery training across different units. A training round that works with both launchers would let the service use the same system in more than one kind of unit, rather than tying the exercise to a single type of vehicle.

The design also addresses environmental flexibility. Lockheed Martin intends J-R-3 to support exercises across different locations and operating conditions. Crews could conduct realistic firing drills during daylight or nighttime training sessions. That is part of the pitch for a purpose-built training round: not only a cheaper rocket, but one that can be used in the varied settings where artillery units actually train.

Sabrina Barnes, vice president of Lockheed Martin Precision Fires Rockets, described affordability as a central design priority. “Our new training round offers a scalable, very affordable solution built on mature rocket motor technology,” she said. She added that the twenty-four-round launch pod addresses the Army’s demand for greater training volume, and that the system aims to deliver that capacity without compromising performance.

That combination, higher volume and lower cost, is the heart of the program as Lockheed Martin presents it. A reusable pod with reloadable tubes is meant to make it practical to fire more training rounds. Mature motor technology is meant to keep the price of those rounds down. And the marking payload is meant to keep the training useful, so that a cheaper exercise is still a serious one.

J-R-3 remains under development. The three successful demonstrations provide evidence of the system’s flight performance, but the Army’s eventual adoption will depend on further development and testing. Further evaluation will determine how effectively J-R-3 meets military training requirements. A successful flight test, even a third one, is not the same thing as a decision to field the system. Interesting Engineering notes that Lockheed Martin is moving closer to a potential replacement for the Army’s existing practice rockets, and that is the right way to hear this news: as progress in a program that is still being proven.

There is also a longer-term idea attached to the work. Lockheed Martin says J-R-3’s modular design could offer applications beyond conventional training exercises. The company says the architecture could support future efforts involving inexpensive, mass-producible rockets. A modular approach could allow developers to adapt existing components for different requirements. That possibility makes the program relevant beyond its immediate replacement objective. At the same time, Lockheed Martin has not announced specific operational applications for such future variants. For now, that is a capability the company says the design could support, not a new weapon it has formally introduced.

The central proposition remains straightforward. Give artillery crews more opportunities to practice without consuming expensive operational rockets. The reusable launch pod and the established motor technology form the foundation of that approach. If further testing bears it out, J-R-3 could help the Army run more frequent live-fire exercises, on a training round built for that job, and do so without drawing as heavily on the costly ammunition reserved for operations.
