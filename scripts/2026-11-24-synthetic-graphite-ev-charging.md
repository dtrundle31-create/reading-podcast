New synthetic graphite paves way for rapid EV charging. This article comes from TechXplore, written by Rachel Leeson of Rice University, and was published on November 23rd, 2026.

Lithium-ion batteries are the kind found in electric cars, cell phones, and cameras. Many of them use anodes made of graphite. The anode is the part of the battery that takes in lithium ions when the battery is charging. During a charge, those lithium ions move into the graphite. How quickly they can move through that graphite is one of the main things that limits how fast a battery can charge.

James Tour's lab at Rice University has developed a new kind of synthetic graphite that supports much faster charging, and that can be produced in a fraction of a second. Tour is the T.T. and W.F. Chao Professor of Chemistry at Rice, and he is the corresponding author on the study.

"This method could help make future electric vehicle batteries charge faster while reducing the cost and environmental impact of battery manufacturing," Tour said. "Our analysis suggests it could reduce energy consumption and greenhouse gas emissions by more than 80% compared with conventional graphite production."

The study was recently published in the journal Advanced Materials. It describes a method that uses a small amount of a nickel compound and an extremely short, powerful electrical pulse. The nickel compound is added to petroleum coke, which is a low-cost carbon residue left over from oil refining. A single electrical pulse then briefly heats the mixture to about 2,500 degrees Celsius. That pulse triggers a rapid transformation into graphite, the same form of carbon found in pencil leads. In this case, the process produces highly ordered, low-defect graphite with promising properties for fast-charging battery anodes.

To understand what highly ordered and low-defect mean here, it helps to picture how graphite is built. Graphite is a two-dimensional material, which means it is made from layers of horizontal structures only one atom thick, stacked on top of each other. In each of those layers, carbon atoms are neatly arranged into hexagons. When the research team added a little bit of the nickel compound to the petroleum coke, the carbon atoms were able to quickly organize around tiny nickel particles.

Haojie Zhu, a postdoctoral associate and the first author of the paper, described the nickel's role this way: "The nickel acts almost like a temporary construction worker, helping the carbon atoms organize themselves into a highly ordered, layered graphite structure. Usually, creating graphite requires prolonged heating at extremely high temperatures for long periods, but this method allows us to do it in just two-tenths of a second."

That speed is one of the central claims of the work. Conventional graphite production, as the researchers describe it, involves long periods of intense heat. This approach uses a flash of electricity lasting two-tenths of a second, or 0.2 seconds, and a brief spike in temperature to about 2,500 degrees Celsius. The starting material is petroleum coke, a cheap leftover from refining oil. The article does not spell out further details of conventional industrial graphite manufacturing beyond that contrast, but Tour's group says its own analysis points to a reduction of more than 80% in both energy use and greenhouse gas emissions compared with the usual way of making graphite.

The nickel does more than help the carbon atoms line up. It also slightly expanded the spacing between the layers in the graphite. That wider gap gave lithium ions greater access when they later entered the material during charging. In a battery, that matters because those ions have to slip in between the graphite sheets. If the sheets are packed too tightly, or if the structure is messy and full of defects, the ions have a harder time moving in quickly. A slightly more open, more orderly structure can make that movement easier, which is part of why faster charging becomes possible.

When the team tested this nickel-formed graphite in a lithium-ion battery, they found that it significantly outperformed conventional graphite for charging. It tolerated a rate equivalent to a four-minute charging time. It also held up over repeated use. After 1,000 charge and discharge cycles, in which the battery was fully drained and then charged up again, the material retained 80% of its capacity. Capacity, here, is how much charge the battery can still hold. Keeping 80% of that after a thousand full cycles is the figure the article reports as evidence that the material does not just charge quickly once, but can keep working after a long stretch of hard use.

Tour put the two sides of the work together: "This method addresses two major challenges simultaneously: improving battery fast-charging performance and simplifying the production of a key battery material. Imagine charging your battery in four minutes."

An important follow-up finding is that the benefits did not depend on leaving nickel in the finished graphite. The team found that the advantages of nickel-assisted production remained even after more than 99% of the residual nickel was removed. That suggests the main benefit comes from how the nickel helps build the graphite structure, not from nickel that stays behind in the material. In Zhu's earlier metaphor, the nickel is a temporary construction worker: it helps organize the carbon, and then most of it can be taken out.

Temperature, it turned out, had to be chosen carefully. Surprisingly, more heat did not necessarily mean better graphite. At excessively high temperatures, much of the nickel was lost. At lower temperatures, the graphite was less ordered. Tour said, "We identified the Goldilocks region for graphite preparation." The phrase points to a middle range that is neither too hot nor too cool: hot enough for the carbon to form well-ordered graphite with the nickel's help, but not so hot that the nickel disappears before it can do that job. The article does not give a narrower temperature window than the roughly 2,500 degrees Celsius of the electrical pulse, but it does make clear that simply turning the heat up further is not the answer.

Zhu returned to the speed of the process, and to the idea that the right conditions matter more than simply using more energy or more time. "What I find especially exciting is that a process lasting just 0.2 seconds can produce graphite with such impressive fast-charging performance," Zhu said. "Making better materials is not always about using more energy or processing them for longer but about applying the right conditions at exactly the right moment."

Taken together, the work is a lab demonstration of a new way to make synthetic graphite for battery anodes. The starting point is petroleum coke, a low-cost leftover from oil refining. A small amount of a nickel compound is added, and a single, powerful electrical pulse heats the mixture to about 2,500 degrees Celsius for two-tenths of a second. The result is highly ordered, low-defect graphite with slightly wider gaps between its atomic layers. In battery tests, that graphite handled a charging rate equivalent to four minutes and retained 80% of its capacity after a thousand full charge and discharge cycles. The researchers also report that more than 99% of the leftover nickel can be removed without losing those benefits, and that their analysis suggests the method could cut energy use and greenhouse gas emissions by more than 80% compared with conventional graphite production. Tour presents it as a possible path to faster-charging electric vehicle batteries that are also cheaper and less environmentally costly to manufacture. The article does not say the material is already in commercial batteries or on a production line.

The paper is titled "Trace-Metal-Assisted Flash Synthesis for Fast-Charging Graphite Anodes," and it was published in Advanced Materials in 2026. Haojie Zhu is the first author, and James Tour is the corresponding author.
