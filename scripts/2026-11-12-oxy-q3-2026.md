Welcome to this earnings brief for Occidental Petroleum, ticker O-X-Y, often called Oxy. This episode covers the company's third-quarter results for twenty twenty-six. Occidental released the numbers on Monday, November ninth, twenty twenty-six, after the close, and held its conference call on Tuesday, November tenth. On the call were Babatunde Cole, vice president of investor relations; Richard Jackson, president and chief executive officer; Sunil Mathew, senior vice president and chief financial officer; and Ken Dillon, senior vice president and president of international oil and gas operations.

This brief is based on the Motley Fool transcript of that call, the company's official earnings release, and contemporaneous coverage, including published consensus estimates and the after-hours stock reaction. I will cover the results against expectations, the debt and capital outlook, management's key points, notable analyst questions, and what to watch next.

Here is the headline. Occidental beat on adjusted earnings, generated a large amount of cash, and reached a debt-reduction milestone it had been working toward all year. Adjusted earnings from continuing operations were one dollar and ninety-one cents a share, on adjusted income of two point zero billion dollars. Reported earnings were one dollar and sixty-one cents a share, on net income attributable to common stockholders of one point six billion dollars. The difference, the company said, mainly reflects derivatives losses, including mark-to-market items in marketing and crude hedges. TipRanks, citing The Fly, put the consensus for adjusted earnings at one dollar and forty-seven cents. Other recaps described a consensus cluster of about one dollar and forty-three to one dollar and forty-seven cents. On any of those published figures, one dollar and ninety-one cents was a substantial beat.

Cash generation was just as important as the per-share number. Occidental generated four point one billion dollars of operating cash flow from continuing operations, and two point eight billion dollars of free cash flow before working capital from continuing operations, after capital expenditures of one point three billion dollars. It produced one million, four hundred twenty-seven thousand barrels of oil equivalent per day globally, exceeding the midpoint of guidance. The Motley Fool takeaway of the call put that beat at seven thousand barrels of oil equivalent per day above the midpoint. Midstream and marketing pre-tax adjusted income exceeded the high end of guidance, the company said, even though the raw pre-tax income line fell sharply from the prior quarter because of items affecting comparability.

The debt headline is the one Jackson put first. In the third quarter, Occidental repaid one point eight billion dollars of principal debt, bringing year-to-date prepayments to ten point four billion dollars, and reduced principal debt to the ten point zero billion dollar milestone. The company then established a new target of six billion dollars of principal debt. Jackson said, quote, We reached an important milestone in the third quarter, reducing principal debt to ten point zero billion dollars after retiring ten point four billion dollars of debt since the beginning of this year, end quote. He also said that with a stronger balance sheet, Occidental is advancing a plan to add more than four point zero billion dollars in annual sustainable cash flow by twenty thirty, providing a pathway to accelerate capital returns and deliver durable long-term value. Management said that upon reaching the six billion dollar debt milestone, the interest-expense run rate would be about four hundred million dollars a year, which is one billion dollars lower than twenty twenty-five interest expense.

Production and operations were stronger in the onshore businesses than in the Gulf of America. New Mexico production was two hundred eighty-four thousand barrels of oil equivalent per day, a twenty-three percent increase since January twenty twenty-six and a new record for the asset. The Powder River Basin grew seventy percent compared with the first quarter, helped by bringing a record twenty-seven wells online. The company said nine thousand wells, or sixty-eight percent of artificial-lift wells, are under artificial-intelligence surveillance, which management reported contributes to a seven percent annual production uplift. New well costs improved seven percent year to date. Domestic lease operating expense was eight dollars and fifty-four cents per barrel of oil equivalent, outperforming quarterly guidance despite higher commodity-linked costs for carbon dioxide and diesel. Unconventional wells from twenty twenty-five and twenty twenty-six were said to have performed at least ten percent above the industry average on a six-month cumulative oil per lateral foot basis. In the Barnett formation, the company cited drilling costs of less than seven hundred fifty dollars per foot, with six-month oil productivity averaging twelve point five barrels of oil per foot. An enhanced-oil-recovery sidetrack program has drilled eighty sidetrack wells year to date, adding ten thousand barrels of oil equivalent per day of production capacity. The company also said it has identified four hundred fifty million barrels of oil equivalent of waterflood resources at a breakeven price of less than forty dollars a barrel.

The Gulf of America was the soft spot. Production there was one hundred ten thousand barrels of oil equivalent per day, affected by a third-party gas pipeline constraint and maintenance activities. The release said those issues caused a temporary curtailment at certain Gulf of America facilities, and that strength in the Permian and Rockies offset that curtailment at the corporate level.

Prices help explain some of the oil and gas income movement. Pre-tax income from oil and gas was two point six billion dollars, compared with two point eight billion dollars in the second quarter. Excluding items affecting comparability, the decrease was driven primarily by lower realized crude prices, partly offset by higher domestic natural gas prices. Third-quarter average W.T.I. and Brent marker prices were eighty-five dollars and seventy-nine cents, and ninety-one dollars and sixty-two cents, per barrel. Average worldwide realized crude oil prices fell eleven percent from the previous quarter, to eighty-five dollars and ninety-one cents a barrel. Realized natural gas liquids prices were twenty-four dollars and sixty-three cents a barrel, flat quarter to quarter. Average domestic realized gas prices improved to one dollar and sixty-one cents per thousand cubic feet, compared with a negative one dollar and forty-eight cents in the previous quarter, a swing the company put at two hundred nine percent.

Midstream and marketing reported pre-tax income of fifty-five million dollars, compared with one point three billion dollars in the previous quarter. That drop is not the operating run rate. The company said that, excluding items affecting comparability, midstream and marketing exceeded the high end of guidance, and that the quarter-over-quarter decrease was driven by lower gas margins from a narrowing of Waha-to-Gulf-Coast spreads and by lower crude margins related to the timing of crude sales, partly offset by higher sulfur volumes and prices at Al Hosn. Equity-method income from W.E.S. was one hundred twenty-one million dollars.

On capital and outlook, third-quarter capital expenditures were one point three billion dollars, and the company said full-year twenty twenty-six spending remains within original guidance ranges. For twenty twenty-seven, management outlined five point nine billion dollars of planned spending, which it expects will maintain relatively flat production compared with twenty twenty-six levels. That is a maintenance-and-cash-flow plan more than a growth plan. Coverage of the call also cited fourth-quarter production guidance of one point four three million to one point four seven million barrels of oil equivalent per day. I will treat that range as reported from the call materials and subsequent recaps, consistent with a company that just produced one point four two seven million barrels a day and is not trying to run away from that level.

The low-carbon businesses got a progress update, not a new earnings engine. The STRATOS direct-air-capture plant has captured two thousand metric tons of atmospheric carbon dioxide to date during commissioning, with full plant start-up scheduled for the end of twenty twenty-six. The company also said it has signed offtake agreements for two million metric tons per year of anthropogenic carbon dioxide, representing fifteen percent of its twenty twenty-six purchased carbon dioxide. Those are real milestones, and they are still small next to a company that just produced one point four million barrels of oil equivalent a day and generated four point one billion dollars of operating cash flow in a single quarter. For now, STRATOS is a completion and start-up watch item at year-end, not a reason this quarter beat estimates.

Now to the question-and-answer session, from the Motley Fool transcript.

Neil Mehta of Goldman Sachs, Doug Leggate of Wolfe Research, Betty Jiang of Barclays, Nitin Kumar of Mizuho, James West of Melius Research, and Arun Jayaram of J.P. Morgan were among the analysts on the call.

Those operational details are worth a slower pass, because they are how Occidental is trying to hold production flat in twenty twenty-seven without starving the asset base. A seven percent new-well cost improvement, a seven percent reported production uplift from artificial-intelligence surveillance on most artificial-lift wells, and sidetrack work that has already added ten thousand barrels a day are efficiency claims, not new basins. The New Mexico record and the seventy percent Powder River Basin growth versus the first quarter show that the onshore machine is still doing heavy lifting. The four hundred fifty million barrels of waterflood resource at a less-than-forty-dollar breakeven, if it is developed on that basis, is a longer-dated inventory item that fits a sustainable-cash-flow plan better than a high-price growth spurt. The Gulf of America constraint is the reminder that not every barrel is in Occidental's control. A third-party gas pipeline and maintenance work were enough to cap that business at one hundred ten thousand barrels a day in the quarter.

The first theme in the question-and-answer session was capital returns and the new six billion dollar debt target. Analysts wanted to know how quickly Occidental can get from ten billion dollars of principal debt to six billion, and what that means for buybacks or a higher dividend once the interest-expense run rate falls toward four hundred million dollars a year. Jackson's framing was that the ten billion dollar milestone is already done, that the next six billion dollar target supports a plan to add more than four billion dollars of annual sustainable cash flow by twenty thirty, and that a stronger balance sheet is the path to accelerating capital returns. He did not turn that into a new, dated buyback program on the call excerpts I am relying on, and I will not invent one. The capital-return story is now a balance-sheet story first, and the six billion dollar debt target is the next milepost on that path from here now.

The second theme was how to grow, or not grow, in twenty twenty-seven. A five point nine billion dollar capital plan that holds production relatively flat is a deliberate choice. Analysts asked whether that leaves room for high-return work in New Mexico, the Powder River Basin, enhanced oil recovery, and Gulf of America waterfloods, without breaking the cash-flow plan. Jackson said the teams have done a good job thinking not just about finding costs and timing, but about longer-term finding and development costs, and about making sure activity fits the sustainable cash-flow plan as the company thinks about twenty thirty. Ken Dillon's international operations and the domestic records in New Mexico and the Powder River Basin were the examples underneath that answer.

The third theme was differentials, gas prices, and midstream. Betty Jiang of Barclays and Nitin Kumar of Mizuho were in the mix of questions on how the commodity tape, not only volumes, shaped the beat. The swing from negative domestic gas realizations in the second quarter to one dollar and sixty-one cents in the third was a gift to the result, and the narrowing of Waha-to-Gulf-Coast spreads worked the other way in midstream. Realized oil, at eighty-five dollars and ninety-one cents a barrel, was down eleven percent from the prior quarter even with W.T.I. averaging eighty-five dollars and seventy-nine cents, so this was not a simple bid-price windfall in crude. Analysts asked how much of the midstream and gas move is lasting, and how much was timing. Management pointed to the fact that midstream still exceeded the high end of guidance on an adjusted basis, and that crude-sale timing and sulfur volumes at Al Hosn were part of the mix. One point three billion dollars of second-quarter midstream pre-tax income shrinking to fifty-five million dollars is a warning not to annualize either quarter without adjustments.

Doug Leggate of Wolfe Research and James West of Melius Research, among others, also went after the longer cash-flow math: how a relatively flat production profile in twenty twenty-seven, a five point nine billion dollar capital budget, a six billion dollar debt target, and a four billion dollar sustainable cash-flow addition by twenty thirty are supposed to fit together. Jackson's answer stayed at the plan level. The teams, he said, have been smart about finding and development costs, not only finding costs and timing, and about making sure activity fits the sustainable cash-flow plan. That is a capital-allocation answer, not a new numeric walk from twenty twenty-seven cash flow to twenty thirty.

The fourth theme was cash taxes and discontinued operations after the OxyChem sale. Arun Jayaram of J.P. Morgan asked about outflows in the discontinued-operations line, about four hundred fifty million dollars in the quarter and about one point four billion dollars year to date. Mathew said that of the third-quarter four hundred fifty-five million dollars, around four hundred fifteen million dollars was related to tax, and the balance, about forty to forty-five million dollars, was environmental and transaction costs. On a year-to-date basis, of the one point four billion dollars, around one point two billion dollars was tax, and the rest was again a combination of environmental and transaction costs. That is a large cash item, and it sits beside the two point eight billion dollars of free cash flow from continuing operations. Both can be true, and investors should not ignore either one.

Jackson's closing was brief. He thanked everyone for the questions and their time, and asked them to have a good day.

The stock's immediate reaction was positive but not explosive. An Investing.com recap of the call said shares rose about two point one percent after hours, to fifty-six dollars and ninety-five cents, after the company topped profit forecasts. That is an after-hours print, not a close, and it fits a quarter that beat on adjusted earnings and cash, while also reminding investors that reported earnings were lower than adjusted earnings because of derivatives, and that a large tax outflow still sits in discontinued operations.

It is also worth putting the year-to-date debt reduction in context. Ten point four billion dollars of principal prepayments in nine months is an unusually fast repair job, and it is the reason Occidental can now talk about a six billion dollar target and a four hundred million dollar interest-expense run rate. That repair was funded by operations, asset-sale follow-through, and a commodity tape that, even with an eleven percent drop in realized oil from the second quarter, still featured W.T.I. in the mid-eighties. If prices weaken materially, the path from ten billion dollars of debt to six billion dollars gets slower, and the four billion dollar sustainable cash-flow ambition for twenty thirty gets harder. If prices hold, the company has already shown it can retire debt faster than many investors probably expected at the start of the year.

The share count, hedges, and discontinued-operations taxes all affect how much of that cash reaches common shareholders. Reported earnings of one dollar and sixty-one cents, rather than the adjusted one dollar and ninety-one cents, are the mark-to-market reminder. The four hundred fifteen million dollars of third-quarter tax in discontinued operations is the cash reminder. Neither item means the operating beat was imaginary. They do mean the cleanest number in the release, adjusted earnings and free cash flow from continuing operations, has to be read alongside those adjustments.
