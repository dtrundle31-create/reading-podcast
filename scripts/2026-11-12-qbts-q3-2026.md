Welcome to this earnings brief for D-Wave Quantum, ticker Q-B-T-S. This episode covers the company's third-quarter results for twenty twenty-six. The quarter ended on September thirtieth. D-Wave released the numbers on Tuesday, November tenth, twenty twenty-six, and held its conference call the same day. On the call were Alan Baratz, chief executive officer; Greg Golkov, acting chief financial officer; and Kevin Hunt, senior director of investor relations.

This brief is based on the Motley Fool transcript of that call, the company's official earnings release, and contemporaneous coverage, including published consensus estimates and the stock's reaction. I will cover the results against expectations, the lack of numerical guidance, management's key points, notable analyst questions, and what to watch next.

Here is the headline. Revenue beat, earnings missed, bookings were lumpy, and the cash burn rose. Third-quarter revenue was four point five million dollars, up twenty-one percent from a year earlier. Yahoo Finance and other recaps said that was about fourteen percent above consensus, so this was a top-line beat. The loss was wider than estimates. G.A.A.P. net loss was forty-eight point two million dollars, or thirteen cents a share, compared with a loss of one hundred thirty-nine point nine million dollars, or about forty-one cents, a year earlier. The year-over-year improvement is not an operating miracle. Golkov said it was due primarily to a one hundred twenty-two million dollar decrease in non-cash charges related to the remeasurement of warrant liabilities in twenty twenty-five, after the remaining publicly traded warrants were redeemed in November twenty twenty-five. Adjusted earnings measures still missed. Recaps said G.A.A.P. earnings missed a consensus loss of about ten cents, and that an adjusted loss of about eleven cents also missed a ten-cent consensus. Adjusted E.B.I.T.D.A. loss was thirty-seven point seven million dollars, widening by seventeen million dollars year over year, as the company spent more on product development and go-to-market work.

The bookings story depends on the time window you pick. Bookings for the first nine months were thirty-seven point four million dollars, up six hundred eleven percent from a year earlier, and that figure includes a twenty million dollar system sale to Florida Atlantic University. Third-quarter bookings, though, were only one point nine million dollars, down nineteen percent year over year, because of the timing of large transactions. Golkov was explicit that bookings in any single quarter can vary significantly based on that timing. Remaining performance obligations, or R.P.O., were thirty-eight point three million dollars, up one thousand two hundred twenty-one percent from two point nine million dollars a year earlier. He said fifty-nine percent of that balance is expected to be recognized as revenue within twelve months after September thirtieth, and seventy-one percent over the twenty-four months following that date. The sales pipeline's dollar value increased more than seventy-five percent from the end of the second quarter, and has more than tripled since the end of twenty twenty-five, driven by interest in system sales.

The company does not provide numerical revenue guidance. Baratz said so directly when asked about twenty twenty-seven. What he would say is that he expects system sales, quantum compute as a service, professional services, and partner sales all to grow in twenty twenty-seven over what the company expects by the end of twenty twenty-six. The near-term revenue watch item is more specific. Golkov said D-Wave expects to deliver the Florida Atlantic University system in the fourth quarter and to recognize a substantial portion of the related revenue then, with an additional portion recognized over the installation period, typically several months after delivery, which in that case would likely be early twenty twenty-seven, plus smaller recurring streams for maintenance and cloud access over several years. If that delivery happens on that schedule, the fourth quarter should be much larger than the third. If it slips, the lumpy pattern continues.

The revenue mix is shifting toward services and enterprise work, which helped growth and hurt margin. Subscription revenue from the Leap cloud service, sometimes described as quantum compute as a service, was two point two million dollars, up fifty-six percent year over year. Professional services revenue was two point zero million dollars, up three hundred eighteen percent, helped by a Fortune one hundred engagement and gate-model research projects. System sales and other revenue were only three hundred sixty thousand dollars, down one point five million dollars year over year because of delivery timing. Commercial revenue was fifty-two percent of the total, up from thirty-one percent a year earlier. G.A.A.P. gross margin fell to fifty-two point nine percent from seventy-one point four percent, and non-G.A.A.P. gross margin was sixty-three point six percent, down fourteen point one points, primarily because professional services carry a lower margin than the mix a year ago.

Costs rose sharply. G.A.A.P. operating expenses were fifty-five point nine million dollars on the call, up eighty-four percent, or twenty-five point five million dollars, from a year earlier. Golkov broke that increase into twelve point three million dollars of personnel costs, four point one million dollars of stock-based compensation, four point six million dollars of depreciation and amortization, one point one million dollars of third-party professional fees, and one point one million dollars of marketing expenses. Non-G.A.A.P. adjusted operating expenses were forty point five million dollars, up seventy-two percent. Operating cash use for the nine months ended September thirtieth was one hundred nine million dollars, compared with fifty-three point six million dollars a year earlier. Cash and marketable securities were five hundred three point four million dollars, down forty percent year over year, primarily because of about two hundred fifty-three million dollars used to acquire Quantum Circuits in January twenty twenty-six. The company also executed a definitive agreement with the U.S. Department of Commerce for access to up to one hundred million dollars of CHIPS and Science Act funding, in installments upon satisfying specified milestones. No CHIPS Act funds had been received as of quarter-end.

Baratz framed D-Wave as the only dual-platform quantum company, with annealing systems in use today and fault-tolerant gate-model systems in development. On the commercial side, he said NTT DOCOMO, Japan's largest mobile operator, deployed its second production application powered by D-Wave's hybrid quantum technology, reducing daily peak location-registration signals by sixty-five point three percent and paging signals by seven point zero percent in the published example, and completing a representative optimization across three hundred thirty-three base stations in about five minutes. He also said the company continues to make progress with other large enterprise customers pursuing production applications, including UnitedHealth Group's Optum and A.T. and T. A Fortune one hundred engagement, first discussed in the first quarter, is a two-year, ten million dollar deal to pursue multiple applications, with the first already in production and others making progress. Partners are becoming more important. C.G.I., after a year of collaboration, plans to integrate D-Wave's platforms into its technology-services portfolio, with an expected focus on logistics, transportation, and retail. At a sold-out user conference in Seoul, the company also announced a memorandum of understanding with L.G. C.N.S., a technology arm of the L.G. Group, focused on enterprise optimization inside the group and among that firm's customers in South Korea and beyond. Baratz said enterprises need help identifying the right problems, developing applications, and integrating them into existing operations, and that partners who already understand those customers can expand D-Wave's reach.

Manufacturing is part of the same story. D-Wave extended its long-standing relationship with SkyWater through a multiyear agreement for semiconductor process development and manufacturing. A proof-of-technology project that used D-Wave's annealing systems to optimize maintenance schedules at SkyWater's fab reduced lot delays by up to thirty percent, according to Baratz, and that work is now advancing to a proof of concept that will add resource availability, technician constraints, and other fab-specific limits. SkyWater has been a partner for more than ten years. Baratz also said prudent supply-chain management requires diversification, so the company will continue to qualify additional manufacturing sources.

On the product side, the company announced performance gains to its Strive hybrid solver, available through the Leap cloud service. On assembly-line balancing benchmark problems, Baratz said the enhanced solver found better solutions in fifteen seconds than the previous version found in one hundred twenty seconds. He also pointed to scientific work on the annealing systems, including a preprint on analog-digital quantum computation and an October preprint describing experiments that realized topological insulators in one, two, and three dimensions using more than seven hundred qubits, which the company believes include the first programmable quantum-dynamics experiments in three dimensions.

The annealing hardware roadmap has two scaling technologies: superconducting interconnects for multichip systems, and scalable input-output control circuitry for much larger processors. Baratz said the company has already shown experimentally that it can achieve strong coupling between prototype annealing fabrics interconnected across two chips, has completed the design of a dual-chip annealing processor, and was releasing that design for fabrication in the week of the call. He also said D-Wave was on track to complete fabrication of its first scalable input-output development wafers in November. those steps are toward larger systems on the roadmap, including twenty-thousand-qubit and, ultimately, one-hundred-thousand-qubit annealing systems. The next-generation Advantage Three system, he later told analysts, is intended to be a multichip solution, scaling by interconnecting multiple processor chips rather than only by putting more qubits on a single chip.

On systems, D-Wave delivered Italy's first annealing quantum computer, a four-thousand-four-hundred-plus-qubit Advantage Two system, for installation at Villa Saporiti in Como, funded by Swiss Quantum Technology, to support Q-Alliance efforts in Europe. The company also has a multiyear semiconductor process and manufacturing agreement with SkyWater, extending a ten-year technology partnership. On the gate-model roadmap, D-Wave launched a simulator beta program in early October for error-aware programming, built around the company's dual-rail superconducting technology. Baratz said that architecture features built-in error detection, which is designed to reduce the hardware overhead required for quantum error correction. Beta participants include both commercial and research organizations, among them B.B.V.A., FirstQFM, Florida Atlantic University, and the Jülich Supercomputing Centre, He said the company is on track to complete a seventeen-qubit gate-model system and is already discussing seventeen-qubit system orders for delivery in twenty twenty-seven. The longer-term roadmap targets one hundred logical qubits capable of reliably executing more than one million operations by twenty thirty-two.

Baratz spent time defining what D-Wave means by a logical qubit, because, in his words, different definitions are floating around, some of them a bit misleading. He said it is generally accepted that a useful fault-tolerant system needs at least one hundred fault-tolerant logical qubits, with fault tolerance defined as the ability to perform at least one million gate operations successfully, and that the company is not aware of any quantum-computing firm that has produced even one logical qubit meeting that standard today. D-Wave's published timeline is to achieve the first fully error-corrected, fault-tolerant logical qubit by twenty twenty-eight, and then one hundred of them by twenty thirty-two. He cautioned investors to clarify other vendors' definitions, some of which, he said, are only error-detected, only partially error-corrected, or below the generally accepted fault-tolerant threshold.

The CHIPS agreement is intended to support both roadmaps: a one-hundred-thousand-qubit annealing system aimed at optimization, materials simulation, blockchain, and artificial intelligence, and a ten-thousand-qubit gate-model system designed to enable those one hundred logical qubits. Baratz also cited third-party analyst placements. He reminded listeners that I.D.C. named D-Wave as one of only two companies in the leaders category in its twenty twenty-six quantum-computing vendor assessment, and that Gartner had recognized the company as a market shaper and as one of only six vendors in the upper-right quadrant of an emerging-market quantum assessment as of November twenty twenty-six. Before handing the call to Golkov, he also recognized two board additions: Bernard Gavgani, former group chief information officer of B.N.P. Paribas, and Kevin Krysler, who joined the board and audit committee.

Golkov added that, in the third quarter, D-Wave recognized revenue from more than one hundred customers. G.A.A.P. gross profit was two point four million dollars, down three hundred thousand dollars, or eleven percent, year over year, while non-G.A.A.P. gross profit was two point nine million dollars, essentially flat. Gross margin, he said, can fluctuate significantly depending on product mix. Third-quarter bookings of one point nine million dollars were down four hundred thirty-five thousand dollars, or nineteen percent, from a year earlier, a decline he again tied to the timing of larger transactions.

Now to the question-and-answer session, from the Motley Fool transcript.

John McPeake of Rosenblatt asked about on-chip qubit-control multiplexing, a bottleneck in the superconducting camp. Baratz said D-Wave's annealing systems today can control more than four thousand qubits with two hundred to three hundred input-output lines, whereas gate-model systems typically require three to five lines per qubit. The company multiplexes control signals and then decodes and routes them on chip, and it is carrying that approach into the gate-model systems. The first chip design with the new scalable input-output control for gate-model systems was out for fabrication, and Baratz said the company expected the chip back in November to begin testing. The goal, he said, is to control one hundred logical qubits with a couple of hundred input-output lines, two hundred to three hundred, and that first returned chip will show whether the company can get there.

William Kingsley Crane of Canaccord Genuity asked about the SkyWater relationship, including intellectual-property protections and whether the partnership had been extended or expanded. Baratz said the SkyWater team had been supportive in working through an agreement that gives D-Wave confidence its intellectual property will be protected and that the skilled SkyWater people on the program will remain part of it. He also said the relationship has expanded in a different direction: D-Wave is now a supplier to SkyWater as well, after the successful proof of technology, with the work moving on to a proof of concept. Crane's follow-up was about the post-election environment and whether support for quantum programs might change. Baratz said he was not seeing any changes in the strength of the pipeline or, perhaps more important, in the sales agreements in negotiation, and that he has seen continued forward momentum.

Kevin Garrigan of Jefferies asked about supply-chain constraints, including dilution refrigerators. Baratz said lead times are longer than the company prefers, that Maybell in the United States is coming on strong and that D-Wave has ordered one of their refrigerators to qualify it, and that with more suppliers he is hopeful timelines may fall rather than lengthen. He also said that as the system-sales pipeline grows and new deals progress, the company is starting to buy ahead for some longer-lead items.

Harsh Kumar of B.M.O. asked Baratz to explain the dual-chip annealing processor and why it matters for getting to twenty thousand or one hundred thousand qubits. Baratz said the current Advantage Two processor has grown qubit count by putting more qubits on a chip, but the next-generation Advantage Three system will be a multichip solution. Two technologies are required: interconnecting processor chips while preserving superconducting and quantum properties, and keeping the number of input-output lines from growing significantly. The company has demonstrated interconnects on sample chips at a small scale and was starting fabrication of its first pair of interconnected actual annealing processor chips, which it will then test as a single integrated processor.

Gary Mobley of StoneX asked, without requesting explicit twenty twenty-seven revenue guidance, how the composition of twenty twenty-seven revenue might change, including presales of seventeen-qubit gate-model systems, partnership-driven sales such as those with C.G.I., more Leap cloud revenue, and government work. Baratz declined to give numbers or breakdowns, then said he expects all of those streams to grow in twenty twenty-seven. Because system sales are much larger bookings, and because their revenue is typically recognized over a shorter period than cloud subscriptions, he said the mix on bookings, and possibly on revenue, may become more significant for system sales. Partners, he said, are just getting started, and twenty twenty-seven is a year when that should start to come online rather than already being a large component. On government, he said D-Wave is seeing more interest from the U.S. government in annealing systems than it has ever seen, with good progress on a couple of fairly significant application opportunities he hopes to discuss in the relatively near future, and that government should become a significantly larger component than it has been.

Tyler, asking for Richard Shannon of Craig-Hallum, asked about NTT DOCOMO's applications, including how often they run and whether the customer developed them on its own or used D-Wave professional services, and also about what was driving the Strive solver's performance. Baratz said NTT DOCOMO developed both production applications on its own, after establishing a strong team, and that both are in production today. Professional services, he said, are a means to an end. The company does not really want to be a large professional-services firm. It wants to sell systems and quantum compute as a service, and it uses professional services to help customers get value from those offerings. The two DOCOMO applications, he added, run relatively infrequently, not on a daily or weekly basis, even though they can make an important operational and financial impact. Most of the Strive improvement, he said, was a rework of the code, with some additional capabilities as well.

Troy Jensen of Cantor Fitzgerald asked whether sovereign-supply-chain pressure, after a user conference in Asia, is showing up globally the way it has in North America. The question broke up at first, and Baratz asked him to repeat it. He then said the European Union, for example, has taken a strong position that E.U. quantum funding can go only to E.U. companies, and that this is starting to spill over into some countries. In Asia, he said, the company is not seeing that same hard line, but is being asked to incorporate local companies into the supply chain for some components on systems delivered there. He is not convinced the industry will ever source everything locally, because some components are simply made better elsewhere, so the supply chain will, by necessity, remain more global. Europe, he said, is a little more challenging, and if country-level money follows the E.U. path, D-Wave would look more to independent or commercial funding, as in the Italy system funded by Swiss Quantum Technology, which he said is not really a government entity.

Krish Sankar of T.D. Cowen asked how much of the one hundred million dollars of CHIPS funding can be allocated to superconducting gate technology, and also about integrating a cryogenic control chip with the annealing systems. Baratz said the first chips that implement the new cryogenic control architecture, designed to work with both annealing and gate-model systems, were due back in November, so testing was just about to begin. On the funding split, he said the work is really on how the company designs, fabricates, and tests the chips, or quantum processing units, and that this work can be applied to both roadmaps more or less equally, with some items more applicable to annealing and some to the gate-model side. Here is what is fair to watch next.

First, whether the Florida Atlantic University system is delivered in the fourth quarter and whether a substantial portion of that twenty million dollar sale is recognized on the expected schedule, with the rest in early twenty twenty-seven.

Second, whether the thirty-eight point three million dollars of remaining performance obligations converts at the fifty-nine percent twelve-month and seventy-one percent twenty-four-month pace Golkov described, and whether the seventy-five percent pipeline increase turns into bookings after a one point nine million dollar bookings quarter.

Third, cash use. Nine-month operating cash use of one hundred nine million dollars, a five hundred three point four million dollar cash and securities balance after the Quantum Circuits acquisition, and a still-undrawn CHIPS award of up to one hundred million dollars are the funding facts.

Fourth, mix and margin. Professional services helped growth and cut gross margin. If system sales rebound in the fourth quarter, the mix should improve. If services remain the growth engine, margins may stay lower.

Fifth, the seventeen-qubit gate-model system, discussions of twenty twenty-seven deliveries, and the longer path to a first fault-tolerant logical qubit by twenty twenty-eight and one hundred of them by twenty thirty-two, alongside the annealing commercial work already in production at customers such as NTT DOCOMO and the Fortune one hundred account.

Sixth, partner and government contributions, which Baratz said are early, with twenty twenty-seven as the year they should start to show up, and with more U.S. government interest in annealing than the company has seen before.
