Welcome to this earnings brief for Quantum Computing Incorporated, ticker Q-U-B-T, often called Q.C.I. This episode covers the company's third-quarter results for twenty twenty-six. The quarter ended on September thirtieth. The company released the numbers on Monday, November ninth, twenty twenty-six, and held its conference call that afternoon. On the call were David Moskowitz, head of investor relations; Yuping Huang, chairman and chief executive officer; and Christopher Bruce Roberts, chief financial officer.

This brief is based on the Motley Fool transcript of that call, the company's press release filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission, and contemporaneous coverage, including published consensus estimates and the stock's next-day reaction. I will cover the results against expectations, the lack of formal guidance, management's key points, notable analyst questions, and what to watch next.

Here is the headline. Revenue jumped, and the loss per share missed. Quantum Computing Incorporated generated nine point five million dollars of third-quarter revenue, compared with three hundred eighty-four thousand dollars a year earlier. That figure also exceeded the nine point two million dollars of revenue the company reported for the entire first half of twenty twenty-six. It beat published estimates. MarketBeat put the consensus at seven point six one million dollars. So this was a clear top-line beat, and a sharp step-up from the company's own recent run rate.

The bottom line went the other way. The company reported a net loss of fifteen point one million dollars, or seven cents a share, compared with net income of two point four million dollars, or one cent a share, a year earlier. MarketBeat said that missed a consensus loss of four cents by three cents. Coverage after the call treated the quarter as a revenue beat and an earnings miss, and the shares gapped down the next session. MarketBeat said the stock opened at six dollars and forty-seven cents, after a prior close of six dollars and eighty-six cents.

The company did not issue formal financial guidance. Roberts was explicit about why. He said the timing of orders and deliveries is difficult to predict, so quarter-to-quarter results are likely to be lumpy, and that the company is not giving guidance at this time. He also said that, over a twelve- to fifteen-month view, management is confident it will have a strong finish to this year and a solid backlog leading into twenty twenty-seven.

Most of the revenue growth came from businesses acquired in the first half of the year. The press release said growth was primarily driven by Luminar Semiconductors, NuCrypt, and NHanced Semiconductors, including photonic components, quantum communications products, and advanced packaging services sold to aerospace, government, and industrial customers. Huang's message was that Q.C.I. is shifting from years of building photonic-based quantum technology to putting those technologies into customers' hands and building a business that can grow sustainably. He said the company has spent years building technology designed for the real world, and that attention is now shifting from what it can build to what it can put to work for customers. That means delivering on commitments and building a business that can grow sustainably. Execution, he said, matters more than ever, and the third-quarter results, in his view, reflect progress in that direction.

Before questions, he came back to a more cautious industry point. He said quantum technology offers tremendous potential, but it is a young industry, and adoption will develop differently across markets and applications. Some customers need photonic technologies today, while others are still exploring what quantum systems can enable as they become more capable and practical. The company, he said, needs to understand those differences and be thoughtful about where it invests. As Roberts outlined, the financial position gives considerable flexibility, particularly in an industry where technology takes time to mature. For Q.C.I., that means advancing the science while building products customers can depend on, growing commercial relationships, improving manufacturing, following through on the roadmap, and being ambitious about what can be achieved while remaining realistic about what it takes to get there.

Gross profit was only four hundred twenty-eight thousand dollars, for a gross margin of four point five percent. That is a thin margin, and management did not pretend otherwise. Roberts said margins remain modest, and that as revenue grows the company sees opportunities to improve manufacturing utilization, increase operating efficiency, and expand margins over time. The company is still in the early innings of integrating acquired factories and filling them.

Operating expenses were twenty-nine point zero million dollars, compared with ten point five million dollars a year earlier. The increase was largely due to higher personnel and related costs from expanded research and development, sales, and marketing, plus about one million dollars of acquisition-related expenses. Interest and other income totaled thirteen point zero million dollars, up from three point five million dollars, mainly from interest on a much larger cash and investment balance. That interest income is a big reason the net loss was not even wider. The press release and the call both make clear that the change from year-ago net income to this quarter's net loss was due largely to higher operating expenses and to smaller mark-to-market gains on a warrant derivative, partly offset by higher revenue and higher interest income.

The balance sheet is the feature that lets the company talk about scale before the margins are healthy. Roberts said Q.C.I. ended the third quarter with approximately one point three billion dollars in cash equivalents and investments, consistent with the end of the second quarter. Total assets were about one point six billion dollars. Total liabilities were forty-seven point seven million dollars. Cash and cash equivalents were one hundred seventy-four point three million dollars, with about one point one billion dollars in investments, and working capital of about nine hundred sixteen point nine million dollars. Management said that liquidity is sufficient to fund operations for at least the next twelve months. Headcount was three hundred thirty employees, up from about three hundred ten at the end of the second quarter.

Contract backlog was thirty-nine point one million dollars, down about two million to three million dollars from the prior quarter, which Roberts attributed to the ordinary difference between contracted orders and recognized revenue. He said backlog typically burns off over twelve to eighteen months, with most of it likely to convert in the next twelve months, but with some more complex products taking longer. That timing variability is also why the company is not guiding quarter by quarter. A backlog that is only about four times this quarter's revenue, and that already declined a little as revenue was recognized, is useful, but it is not a multi-year locked-in book of business. New orders have to keep arriving for the growth to continue.

The first-half comparison is the cleanest way to see the step-up. The company did more revenue in this one quarter than in the first six months of the year combined. That is encouraging, and it is also why a single lumpy quarter should not be extrapolated in a straight line. If a few large aerospace or government deliveries slipped from the third quarter into the fourth, or the other way around, the year could still finish strong, as management expects, without every quarter looking like this one.

On the product side, Huang and the prepared materials emphasized Dirac-three, the company's quantum optimization machine. Capacity was cited at nine thousand nine hundred eighty variables, an increase from previous generations, and the first customer shipment is expected in the fourth quarter of twenty twenty-six. That shipment is one of the few dated product milestones on the call, and it is a useful checkpoint for whether the quantum story is starting to sit beside the acquired photonics revenue, or still mostly behind it. About seventy percent of current revenue is from aerospace and government subcontracts, with a long-term goal of a fifty-fifty commercial split. Getting to that split would require either a lot more commercial work, a change in the government mix, or both, and it will not happen in a single quarter.

The company also pointed to a first pharmaceutical-market collaboration with INmune Bio, a framework agreement with Hamad Bin Khalifa University in Qatar for cloud access to Dirac-three and quantum education, and a benchmark of Dirac-three against the Hexaly classical solver in which management said the quantum system showed better overall performance in time to solution and quality for specific problems. Those are management-reported comparisons and early commercial beachheads, not an independent bake-off I can certify, and not yet a second revenue engine on the scale of the acquired aerospace and government work. They do, however, show how management wants the story to broaden: from components and packaging sold mainly into government supply chains, toward quantum systems and services in new verticals, including pharma and international education and cloud access.

The manufacturing map underneath all of that is still messy, in a way the company itself admits. Eleven facilities in eight states is a lot of footprint for a company that just reported nine point five million dollars of quarterly revenue. The acquisitions were designed to bring in photonics, quantum communications, and advanced packaging, and to speed the Fab Two plan. The test now is whether that footprint gets used more fully, or whether it remains a collection of sites with a four point five percent blended gross margin and one million dollars of leftover acquisition costs in the quarter.

Capital allocation is now a central investor question, because the company has more than a billion dollars to put to work. Roberts said Q.C.I. has earmarked several hundred million dollars for capital expenditure, and that the investment planned for the NHanced Fab Two facility is in the ballpark of seventy million to eighty million dollars. Equipment orders have already begun, and a lot of that spend is expected between now and March thirty-first of next year. He also said the company continues to review deal flow and acquisition opportunities, while wanting enough working capital to ride through periods when raising capital would be highly dilutive. The company has already completed three acquisitions in about eight months.

Huang added that last year's original plan for Fab Two was a greenfield approach, with a later step expected perhaps at the end of twenty twenty-eight, and that the NHanced acquisition has accelerated that roadmap. He said the acquired facilities serve two purposes: supporting the quantum roadmap, including work toward heterogeneous integrated circuits that the team hopes to demonstrate in a twelve- to eighteen-month timeframe, or possibly sooner, and enabling volume production of quantum devices after more than twenty years of research.

Roberts also flagged a risk that did not get a lot of headlines but should be on the list. He said there is a possibility of export restrictions later on, and that I.T.A.R. regulations or other geopolitical factors could complicate future international sales of cutting-edge technology. He also said the bulk of sales and backlog is still domestic, with a few small overseas sales.

Now to the question-and-answer session, from the Motley Fool transcript.

John McPeake of Rosenblatt opened with a question that led Roberts into the lumpiness of revenue and awards. Roberts said there is not much to read into a single-quarter backlog change, that backlog was down about two to three million dollars, and that backlog will fluctuate as the difference between contracted orders and recognized revenue. That set the tone for the rest of the call: this is a lumpy, low-volume, technically complex manufacturing business, not a smooth software subscription curve.

Max Michaelis of Lake Street Capital Markets asked what milestones to watch on commercial growth over the next twenty-four months, and what the bigger hurdles are on the manufacturing-scale side. Roberts repeated that the company is not giving guidance because order and delivery timing is hard to predict, that quarters will be lumpy, and that the signs of progress to watch are a strong finish to the year, a solid backlog into twenty twenty-seven, and evidence that a broader range of customers is validating the technology.

Scott Buck of Milan Partners asked how quickly the thirty-nine point one million dollar backlog converts, and about bringing manufacturing operations together. Roberts said the backlog typically converts over twelve to eighteen months, with most of it likely in the next twelve months and some taking longer because the company is building small numbers of very complex products, where a slip of a couple of weeks can move revenue recognition. On manufacturing, he said the company has operations in eight states and eleven facilities, and that the work is more about integrating the company than about a simple site consolidation, deferring some of the detail to Huang.

Nehal Chokshi of Northland Capital Markets also joined the queue, and Kieran McCabe of Cantor asked about the INmune Bio announcement, the first customer in pharma and biotech, and whether a new chief revenue officer signals a push into industries beyond aerospace and defense. Huang said yes, the company is looking at other industries, including biotech and pharma, and that the strategy is both to go deeper within organizations and to spread within those verticals. The Fab Two follow-up is where he explained that NHanced has accelerated a roadmap that had been a later greenfield plan.

Ed Woo of Ascendiant Capital asked how much of the pipeline is U.S. versus international, and about long-term revenue potential. Roberts said the bulk of sales, backlog, and revenue is still domestic, that the company is looking at international opportunities and has made a few small overseas sales, and that he would not over-specify a single long-term market size on the call. That answer sits next to his later comment about possible export restrictions. International expansion is an opportunity and a policy risk at the same time.

Jesse Sockler of B.T.I.G. asked the integration question most directly. He said a lot of the investment world still sees Luminar Semiconductors, NHanced, NuCrypt, and the core business as four separate businesses, and he asked what the first wave of flagship or integrated products looks like, and on what timeline. Huang said the acquired facilities support the quantum roadmap and have already made possible prototypes that could not have been built before the sites were combined. He said the team hopes to demonstrate some heterogeneous integrated circuits in twelve to eighteen months, perhaps sooner, and that after more than twenty years of quantum research the company now has to solve the scalable manufacturing challenge so it can put products into many customers' hands. Sockler's follow-up was about the capital plan for more than a billion dollars of cash. That produced Roberts's seventy to eighty million dollar Fab Two figure and the comment that a lot of the spend will occur by March thirty-first of next year, while the company continues to evaluate equipment needs and acquisition opportunities and wants to avoid raising capital when it would be highly dilutive.

One more point on expectations, because the miss on earnings can be misread. The seven-cent loss is a real miss against a four-cent consensus, but this is not a company that was expected to be profitable. The disappointment is about the pace of spending and the still-thin margin, not about a failed profitable quarter. The encouragement is about the revenue run rate and the cash. Both can be true at the same time, which is why the next few quarters, not this one quarter, will decide how the story is remembered.

Here is what is fair to watch next.

First, whether fourth-quarter revenue stays strong enough to support management's comment about a strong finish to the year, including the first Dirac-three customer shipment expected in the quarter, without a formal guide to measure it against.

Second, backlog. Thirty-nine point one million dollars is a real foundation, but it declined by a couple of million dollars this quarter, converts over twelve to eighteen months, and will bounce around as orders and deliveries land.

Third, gross margin. Four point five percent is not a finished manufacturing story. The test is whether utilization and integration lift that margin as the acquired factories scale.

Fourth, spending and dilution risk. The company has about one point three billion dollars of liquidity and is planning seventy to eighty million dollars for Fab Two, plus possible further acquisitions, while still expecting additional losses and elevated operating expenses.

Fifth, whether the acquired businesses start to look like one company, especially if Huang's twelve- to eighteen-month goal for heterogeneous integrated circuits is met, and whether the revenue mix can move from about seventy percent aerospace and government toward a more commercial balance.

Sixth, export and international policy risk, which Roberts raised himself, even though most sales are still domestic.

It is also worth keeping the scale in perspective. Nine point five million dollars is a record quarter for this company, and it is still a small manufacturing business measured against a one point three billion dollar cash and investment account and twenty-nine million dollars of quarterly operating expenses. Interest income of thirteen million dollars is doing a lot of work. If rates fall, or if more of the cash is spent on factories and acquisitions, that cushion shrinks. The acquired photonics, packaging, and communications businesses are what made the revenue step-up possible. They are also why gross margin is only four point five percent and why investors, as Sockler said, still struggle to see one company instead of four. The next few quarters should show whether utilization, integration, and Dirac-three shipments start to change those facts, or whether revenue remains lumpy and the equity story remains mostly about the cash pile and the roadmap.

Let me close with a short recap.

Quantum Computing Incorporated beat on revenue, at nine point five million dollars versus consensus of about seven point six million dollars and versus three hundred eighty-four thousand dollars a year earlier, but missed on a seven-cent loss per share against a four-cent estimated loss. Gross margin was only four point five percent, operating expenses jumped to twenty-nine million dollars, and interest income of thirteen million dollars helped cushion the loss. The company held about one point three billion dollars of cash and investments and a thirty-nine point one million dollar backlog, and it did not issue formal guidance because orders and deliveries are lumpy. Management is steering toward manufacturing scale, a seventy to eighty million dollar Fab Two build, and a first Dirac-three shipment in the fourth quarter. Shares gapped down after the miss on earnings, opening the next session at six dollars and forty-seven cents versus a prior close of six dollars and eighty-six cents, according to MarketBeat. That is a modest gap, not a collapse, but it is a reminder that investors were looking through a record revenue quarter to the seven-cent loss, the thin margin, and the still-unguided outlook. The follow-through is whether the revenue step-up is durable, whether Dirac-three actually ships in the fourth quarter, and whether margins and integration start to catch up with the new scale. If they do, this quarter can look like the start of a commercial manufacturing company. If they do not, it can look like a cash-rich holding company that had one strong delivery quarter after a string of acquisitions.
