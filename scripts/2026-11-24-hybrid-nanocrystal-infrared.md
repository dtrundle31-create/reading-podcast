Hybrid nanocrystal surface reveals new way to tune interactions with infrared light. This article comes from Phys.org, written by Patricia DeLacey of the University of Michigan, and was published on November 22nd, 2026.

Researchers have found that a sheet just one crystal thick, made from two kinds of nanocrystals mixed together in a solution, can amplify or suppress infrared light more than you would expect from adding up what each type of crystal does on its own. That extra boost, or extra suppression, is what the team describes as a superlinear optical effect.

The sheet is called a metasurface. In this case, it is a single layer of tiny crystals made of indium oxide, each one doped with a small amount of tin. Doping, here, simply means adding a controlled amount of another element to change how the material behaves. Some of the nanocrystals contain one percent tin. Others contain three percent. Those two kinds of particles are mixed together at random, and then they settle into a layer just one crystal thick. Once they are in that layer, they interact with one another and with incoming infrared light, and those interactions produce the unusual optical behavior.

The experimental results also helped the team build a model that other researchers can use as a design tool. Instead of relying so heavily on trial and error, scientists could use the model to choose doping levels and mixing ratios that produce a particular kind of light-matter interaction.

To understand why the mixed layer behaves this way, it helps to start with what happens when infrared light hits the surface. The incoming light stirs up waves of electrons, called plasmons, on the metasurface. Those electron waves trap light on the nanocrystals themselves and in the tiny gaps between them. The two kinds of crystals do not respond in quite the same way. The mismatched electron waves of neighboring one-percent and three-percent indium tin oxide nanocrystals either boost or suppress that trapped energy, depending on the frequency of the infrared light.

The work was led by researchers in the University of Michigan's College of Engineering, and it was published in the journal A.C.S. Nano. The lead author is Yujin Park, a postdoctoral research fellow in chemical engineering at Michigan. The study was a collaboration that also included researchers from Oak Ridge National Laboratory, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, the University of Texas at Austin, the University of Colorado Boulder, and the Catalan Institution for Research and Advanced Studies, known as ICREA, in Barcelona, Spain.

Park described how the team arrived at a shared explanation. "We used different techniques and different theoretical backgrounds, and even though we studied them independently at first, eventually everything pointed toward a single theory and a single phenomenon," Park said.

The researchers approached the material at several different scales, starting broad and then zooming in. They first noticed the deviations from ordinary, linear optical effects with a far-field infrared spectroscopy technique. In that approach, an infrared light source shines on the material as a whole. The measurement effectively averages out the interactions among all of the tiny crystals, so it can tell you that something unusual is happening, but it does not show you what is going on between individual particles.

To get a closer look, the team used a near-field technique, bringing the light source much closer to the surface. They used a method called synchrotron infrared nanospectroscopy, or SINS, at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory. That allowed them to study how the optical response varied across the metasurface in small clusters of nanocrystals. SINS showed that absorption was not uniform. It changed from spot to spot, depending on each particle's immediate neighbors.

They then zoomed in even further with a powerful electron microscope at Oak Ridge National Laboratory. The electron beam mapped plasmon energy patterns around individual nanocrystals, with subnanometer precision. That work confirmed two important points. First, the one-percent and three-percent indium tin oxide nanocrystals really were mixed at random. Second, neighboring particles interact in ways that change how the material absorbs light.

The difference between the two kinds of crystals comes down to the energy of their plasmon waves. The three-percent tin nanocrystals produce higher-energy plasmon waves than the one-percent crystals. The article compares the effect to two tuning forks of different pitches vibrating in water. Their ripples overlap in the gaps between the nanocrystals, which are about two and a half nanometers wide. At some frequencies of incoming infrared light, those overlapping ripples reinforce each other. At other frequencies, they dampen each other.

Delia Milliron, the James and Judith Street Professor of Chemical Engineering at the University of Michigan and a senior author of the study, pointed out that the electron beam could reveal differences the light measurements could not. "There are things that are invisible to light that become very obvious in the electron beam," Milliron said. "We could readily differentiate the nanocrystals doped with three percent tin from the ones doped with one percent tin based on the way they interact with the electron beam, an interaction that's completely invisible with light."

Thomas Truskett, the Vennema Professor of Chemical Engineering at Michigan and also a senior author, said the experiments lined up with what computer models had already suggested. "The experimental data from this study validate the physical picture predicted from electromagnetic simulations, paving the way for computation-guided engineering of nanocrystal-based plasmonic metasurfaces," Truskett said.

There is a practical reason this approach is interesting beyond the physics itself. These metasurfaces are made in solution. That sidesteps some of the limits of expensive, top-down manufacturing, such as etching microscopic patterns onto a surface, and it could help the technology scale up.

Milliron also looked ahead to how researchers might use these close-range, or near-field, interactions as a design tool. "Moving forward, it's a question of how we use near-field interactions as a further design tool for how metamaterials manipulate light, potentially converting light between frequencies or polarizing light in unusual ways," Milliron said.

The work is still in the early stages of development. Even so, the article notes that ultrathin nanocrystal coatings could eventually open the door to a variety of applications, including antiglare coatings, thermal camouflage, or surfaces that cool themselves with light.

The paper is titled "Collective Plasmon Resonances in Hierarchically Doped Metal-Oxide Nanocrystal Metasurfaces," and it was published in A.C.S. Nano in 2026.
