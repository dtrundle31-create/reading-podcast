US energy firms back 77 MW modular nuclear reactors for commercial grid integration. This article comes from Interesting Engineering, written by Aman Tripathi, and was published on November 23rd, 2026.

A new engineering alliance is aiming to speed up the worldwide rollout of compact nuclear power plants that can run around the clock. Sargent and Lundy has partnered with ENTRA1 Energy to provide design and engineering support for what the companies call ENTRA1 Energy Plants. Those plants, Sargent and Lundy said in a press release, intend to use NuScale Power Corporation’s proprietary small modular reactor technology.

The contrast with conventional nuclear construction is one of the first points the article makes. Traditional nuclear plants require a great amount of land. These new facilities, by contrast, are designed for locations with strict space constraints. Each plant would use NuScale Power Modules, or N-P-Ms, which have a small physical footprint. Their emergency planning zone is also limited strictly to the site boundary. The article presents that as part of the compact design: the planning zone stops at the edge of the site itself.

The modular approach is meant to give operators flexibility as demand grows. Plant capacity can be built in stages. A first reactor unit can generate electricity while crews install additional modules nearby. That is a different construction logic from a traditional large reactor, which generally has to be completed as a single, large project before it can produce power. With this design, the idea is that a plant can start delivering electricity from the first module and then expand, module by module, as power needs increase.

The NuScale Power Module design is based on proven pressurized water-cooled reactor technology. Sargent and Lundy highlighted that the module was developed to supply energy for several purposes, not only for sending electricity to the grid. Those purposes include electrical generation, district heating, desalination, commercial-scale hydrogen production, and other process heat applications. In other words, the same compact reactor is being positioned as a source of both electricity and heat for industrial and municipal uses.

A single unit produces 77 megawatts of carbon-free baseload electricity. Baseload, in this context, means power that is meant to be available around the clock, rather than only when the wind is blowing or the sun is shining. Regulators have approved the modules for both on-grid and off-grid operation. That off-grid approval is part of what makes the story notable. These reactors are not limited to feeding a traditional utility network. They can also be used in settings that are not tied to the commercial grid.

Safety is another central claim. The reactors incorporate fully passive, walk-away safety systems. If an interruption occurs, the modules shut down and self-cool indefinitely. NuScale put that more specifically: N-P-Ms can shut down and self-cool indefinitely with no operator action, no A-C or D-C power, and no additional water. That is the meaning of walk-away safety as the company describes it. The reactor is designed so that, if something goes wrong, it does not depend on operators staying at the controls, or on pumps that need electricity, or on an extra supply of cooling water being added from outside. According to NuScale, the module can shut itself down and keep itself cool on its own, and keep doing so without a time limit.

Beyond supplying the electrical grid, the reactors are engineered to support heavy industry and municipal infrastructure. They can deliver direct process heat for commercial-scale hydrogen production, water desalination, and district heating networks. Process heat is the high-temperature energy that factories and industrial plants often need for their operations, not just the electricity that runs their lights and motors. District heating refers to systems that send heat from a central plant to homes and buildings in a community. Desalination is the process of turning seawater or other salty water into fresh water. Hydrogen production at a commercial scale is another energy-intensive industrial process. The article presents the NuScale modules as machines that can serve all of those jobs, not only as small power plants for the grid.

Leaders from both companies said that delivering these complex, multi-purpose facilities requires early technical alignment. Victor Suchodolski, the chairman, president, and C-E-O of Sargent and Lundy, noted that converting new generation plans into dependable power demands rigorous engineering from the very start. “We bring more than seven decades of nuclear engineering experience to that challenge and look forward to working with ENTRA1 Energy to advance their projects,” he said. Sargent and Lundy is being brought in, in other words, not as a late-stage contractor but as an engineering partner from the beginning of the work.

Charles Peterson, the president of ENTRA1 Energy, welcomed the collaboration as global demand for reliable electricity continues to rise. He noted that drawing on Sargent and Lundy’s deep nuclear background will help his company execute its development pipeline. The article does not list specific plant sites or a construction timetable, but it does describe a company that already has projects it wants to advance, and that is looking to an established nuclear engineering firm to help carry them out.

The partnership builds on an existing commercial arrangement between ENTRA1 and NuScale. As NuScale’s exclusive global strategic partner, ENTRA1 develops, finances, owns, and operates the energy plants. NuScale supplies the certified reactor hardware for these projects. That is a clear division of roles. NuScale is the technology company, providing the approved reactor modules. ENTRA1 is the company that puts the plants together as businesses: developing the projects, arranging the financing, owning the facilities, and operating them once they are built. Sargent and Lundy now enters as the engineering and design partner supporting those plants.

The NuScale Power Module remains the first and only small modular reactor design to hold regulatory approval from the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission. That is a standing the article emphasizes. Other companies are developing compact reactors as well, but according to Interesting Engineering, NuScale’s design is still the only one that has that U.S. regulatory approval. The article says the technology is ready for worldwide deployment, and that it offers traditional nuclear benefits at a lower cost. That lower-cost claim is how the companies and the article present the technology. The piece does not provide a specific price comparison or a completed commercial plant to illustrate it.

By pairing NuScale’s certified reactors with Sargent and Lundy’s engineering pedigree, ENTRA1 aims to deploy scalable nuclear infrastructure across global energy markets. ENTRA1 Energy concluded that the collaboration strengthens its ability to advance projects and meet growing demand for reliable power.

Taken together, the announcement is less about a single new reactor design and more about an attempt to turn an already certified module into working plants. The 77-megawatt NuScale Power Module is the hardware. ENTRA1 is the company that plans to develop, finance, own, and run the facilities. Sargent and Lundy is the engineering firm hired to help design them. The plants are meant to be compact enough for tight sites, expandable one module at a time, and usable either on the grid or off it. They are also being offered as sources of heat for hydrogen production, desalination, and district heating, with a safety system the manufacturer says can shut down and cool itself without operators, electricity, or extra water.

That is the story from Interesting Engineering on the partnership to deploy 77-megawatt modular nuclear reactors.
