Welcome to this earnings brief for Rocket Lab, ticker R-K-L-B. This episode covers the company's third-quarter results for twenty twenty-six. The quarter ended on September thirtieth. Rocket Lab released the numbers on Monday, November ninth, twenty twenty-six, and held its conference call that afternoon. On the call were Sir Peter Beck, founder and chief executive officer; Adam Spice, chief financial officer; and Murielle Baker, director of corporate and launch communications.

This brief is based on the Motley Fool transcript of that call, the company's official earnings release, and contemporaneous coverage of the results, including published consensus estimates and the stock's reaction. I will cover the results against expectations, the fourth-quarter outlook, management's key points, notable analyst questions, and what to watch next.

Here is the headline. It was a mixed quarter in the way Wall Street keeps score, and a strong one in the way the company wants to be judged. Revenue was a record two hundred sixty-seven point eight million dollars, which the release rounded to two hundred sixty-eight million dollars, up seventy-three percent from a year earlier and about thirty-four million dollars higher than the second quarter. That beat both the company's own prior guidance range of two hundred fifty million to two hundred sixty-five million dollars, and published consensus estimates that clustered around two hundred fifty-five to two hundred fifty-seven million dollars. Investing.com put the estimate at two hundred fifty-seven point one seven million dollars. TipRanks used two hundred fifty-seven point two one million dollars. Benzinga used two hundred fifty-five point seven one million dollars.

Earnings missed. The company reported a G.A.A.P. loss of twelve cents a share, compared with an eight-cent loss in the second quarter. Investing.com and TipRanks said the consensus was a loss of seven cents. Benzinga said estimates were for a six-cent loss. The twelve-cent loss missed that cluster. The Motley Fool transcript takeaway said the wider loss was influenced by Iridium transaction costs and debt extinguishment. Operating expenses also ran hot. Non-G.A.A.P. operating expenses were one hundred thirty point two million dollars, above the company's prior guidance range of one hundred twenty-one million to one hundred twenty-seven million dollars. An Investing.com analysis said G.A.A.P. operating expenses were one hundred sixty-nine point three million dollars against guidance of one hundred forty-three million to one hundred forty-nine million dollars, with Neutron development and Iridium deal charges among the drivers.

The backlog is the number Beck put at the center of the story. Total backlog was approximately three point five seven billion dollars, a fifty-two percent sequential increase from about two point four billion dollars at the end of the second quarter. Nearly one billion dollars of that increase came from new launch contracts signed in the third quarter across Electron, HASTE, and Neutron, and the company also signed four hundred seventy million dollars of new Space Systems contracts, up five hundred twelve percent from a year earlier. Year-to-date Space Systems contracts were six hundred eighty-eight million dollars, up three hundred nineteen percent from the same period in twenty twenty-five. The launch backlog rose to one hundred seventeen launches, a new record. Of the current backlog, thirty-two point nine percent is expected to be recognized as revenue within the next twelve months, which means most of the record backlog sits further out.

Beck said, quote, Q three twenty twenty-six was a defining quarter for Rocket Lab, end quote, in the release, and pointed to the combination of record revenue, record backlog, and an end-to-end space strategy in which launch, spacecraft, and components are increasingly sold together. Twelve Rocket Lab-built spacecraft were successfully launched and are now operating, including eight satellite platforms for Globalstar's direct-to-device constellation, twin spacecraft conducting Mars science for NASA, the VICTUS HAZE mission for the United States Space Force, and a spacecraft for Viasat's NASA Communications Services Depot. More than fifty-five spacecraft are now in development and build.

The revenue mix still tilts toward Space Systems. Space Systems revenue was two hundred fourteen point eight million dollars, up thirteen point three percent sequentially, helped by satellite-platform scaling and contributions from recent laser-communications and robotics acquisitions. Launch services revenue was fifty-three point one million dollars, up nineteen percent sequentially, after two additional Electron missions on which full value was recognized at launch. Electron production averaged one rocket every eight work days, which the company said allows it to meet surges in customer demand. Production headcount rose forty percent from the prior quarter, to one thousand seven hundred twenty-eight employees.

Neutron was the strategic center of the launch discussion, and it came with both pricing power and a delay. The company signed eight Neutron launches in the third quarter for approximately five hundred fifteen million dollars, bringing the Neutron backlog to sixteen missions ahead of the rocket's debut. The base launch price on multiple missions signed in the quarter was seventy million dollars, an escalated rate the company tied to tightening global launch supply. An Investing.com analysis said that was up from fifty-five million dollars, a twenty-seven percent increase, and that open slots now start in the twenty thirties. I am treating the seventy million dollar price and the sixteen-mission backlog as company figures, and the comparison with a prior fifty-five million dollar price as published analysis.

The first Neutron launch is now targeted for no earlier than twenty twenty-seven. Critical milestones continue across assembly, integration, testing, and delivery of first-flight hardware to Launch Complex Three. Coverage of the call said first flight slipped after composite voids were found in the first-stage tank, requiring replacement and cryogenic retesting, and that management was not ready to revise its one-three-five launch cadence plan. I will be careful here. The official release uses the "no earlier than twenty twenty-seven" language. The tank-void explanation appears in contemporaneous coverage of the call, and it is one of the first things to confirm in the transcript's technical discussion rather than to over-specify beyond what the company itself put in the release.

Margins were a bright spot. Non-G.A.A.P. gross margin was thirty-eight point four percent, above management's prior guidance range of thirty-five to thirty-seven percent, thanks to favorable product mix and absorption. G.A.A.P. research and development expense was ninety-eight point four million dollars, up sixteen million dollars sequentially, primarily for Neutron Flight One development and engine production. Capital expenditures were thirty-one point one million dollars, including Neutron infrastructure such as the recovery barge the company refers to as the Return on Investment barge.

Cash flow improved sharply, even if free cash flow was not yet positive. G.A.A.P. operating cash flow was a positive seven point two million dollars, compared with a use of eighty-four point one million dollars in the second quarter, driven by milestone payments on satellite programs. Non-G.A.A.P. free cash flow was a use of twenty-three point nine million dollars, an improvement from a use of one hundred ten point one million dollars in the second quarter. Liquidity was four point seven billion dollars, including two point four billion dollars of gross proceeds from an at-the-market equity offering completed in September. That offering fully funded the pending acquisition of Iridium Communications. The company also said it received approval from Iridium lenders to maintain Iridium's credit facility after the transaction, and obtained overwhelming approval from Iridium shareholders. Regulatory approval remains the outstanding item.

Fourth-quarter guidance is for another record revenue quarter, with a profitability outlook that was a bit weaker than some estimates. Rocket Lab expects revenue of two hundred seventy million to two hundred ninety million dollars, or about four point five percent sequential growth at the midpoint. That midpoint of two hundred eighty million dollars is above the analyst consensus Investing.com and TipRanks put near two hundred sixty-five million dollars. G.A.A.P. gross margins are guided to thirty-four to thirty-six percent, and non-G.A.A.P. gross margins to thirty-nine to forty-one percent. G.A.A.P. operating expenses are guided to one hundred sixty-two million to one hundred sixty-eight million dollars, and non-G.A.A.P. operating expenses to one hundred thirty-six million to one hundred forty-two million dollars. Net interest income is guided at forty-five million dollars. Adjusted E.B.I.T.D.A. is guided to a loss of fourteen million to twenty million dollars. TipRanks noted that the consensus adjusted E.B.I.T.D.A. estimate was a loss of about twelve point six million dollars, so the guided loss is wider than that estimate. The company also guided to six hundred eighty-one million basic weighted-average common shares outstanding, including about forty-one million Series A convertible participating preferred shares.

Now to the question-and-answer session, from the Motley Fool transcript.

The call drew a long analyst line: Andres Sheppard of Cantor Fitzgerald, Edison Yu of Deutsche Bank, John Godyn of Citigroup, Jan Engelbrecht of Baird, Ronald Epstein of Bank of America, Trevor Walsh of Citizens, Kristine Liwag of Morgan Stanley, Gautam Khanna of T.D. Securities, Andre Madrid of B.T.I.G., Jeff Van Rhee of Craig-Hallum, Michael Leshock of KeyBanc Capital Markets, Erik Rasmussen of Stifel, Anthony Valentini of Barclays, Suji Desilva of Roth Capital, and Ryan Koontz of Needham.

Andres Sheppard of Cantor Fitzgerald asked about the backlog increase and about Neutron pricing. Spice's answer is worth quoting closely. He said, just to be clear, the Neutron pricing isn't going to seventy million dollars, it is at seventy million dollars, and that the company has already put some of that pricing into backlog. That distinction matters. This is not a future price target. It is a current booked price on missions already signed.

John Godyn of Citigroup congratulated the company on reaching one hundred Electron launches faster than Falcon nine did, while noting that Falcon nine later scaled to around one hundred seventy launches a year. Beck said Electron is not a reusable vehicle, and that the big unlock for huge cadence is reusability, even as the factory has already pushed production to a high rate. On a supply-chain follow-up about the large number of satellites in build, Beck said Rocket Lab is so vertically integrated that the things it worries about are different from those of companies that do not control as much of their own supply chain.

Jan Engelbrecht of Baird asked a broader competitive question. Beck said that, fundamentally, two commercial companies have developed a vehicle, brought it to market, and scaled: SpaceX and Rocket Lab. Spice added that he does not think a roughly twenty percent launch growth rate is at risk, given the demand the company has been talking about, and that on HASTE and related hypersonic-test work, the company does not see cancellation risk, describing those programs as pretty bipartisan and long-standing.

Ronald Epstein of Bank of America asked about the tight launch market. Beck said he was hoping someone was not going to ask that question, then said launch capacity is incredibly constrained, and that in many respects Rocket Lab is rooting for everybody because the constraint is industry-wide. Spice said rising prices for Electron are certainly helping, and that getting deposits in for Neutron launches is helping, but that nothing is really going to permanently change the cash profile by itself in the way some investors might hope from price alone.

Trevor Walsh of Citizens asked where development effort is going. Beck said the work has been all around how to increase launch cadence faster, primarily around engines and production. Spice noted that Space Systems, now by far the largest part of the business, is not overly research-and-development intensive, and that the company will continue to invest where it must for Neutron and related launch capability.

Michael Leshock of KeyBanc asked about pad capacity. Beck said there are actually two pads side by side, so capacity is higher than a single-pad image would suggest, because the company can turn launches as people have seen. Suji Desilva of Roth Capital asked a deal-structure question; Spice said it is a tricky question, and that you kind of build the deal you want when it is put in front of you, and that a deal had not been put in front of the company that was enough to take a much different shape. Ryan Koontz of Needham asked about contract quality. Beck said none of these contracts are, in his words, janky contracts with silly things in them, that customers do a lot of due diligence, and that the company has long-standing relationships.

The questions clustered around five topics.

First, whether the backlog is real and how fast it converts. Only thirty-two point nine percent of backlog is expected to come into revenue in the next twelve months, so analysts wanted to understand how much of the three point five seven billion dollars is near-term versus Neutron and later Space Systems work. Management said it felt very good about the state of the backlog, and pointed to the breadth of new launch and spacecraft awards.

Second, Neutron pricing and timing. The seventy million dollar base price and the sixteen-mission backlog were treated as evidence of pricing power in a tight launch market. The offset was the "no earlier than twenty twenty-seven" debut and the work still required on first-flight hardware. Analysts wanted to know whether the delay changes the early flight cadence, and management, according to later coverage, was not ready to rewrite the one-three-five plan on the call.

Third, the Iridium transaction. The deal is financed, lenders have agreed to keep the credit facility in place, and Iridium shareholders have approved it. The remaining gate is regulatory approval. Analysts asked whether that process is on track. Management called progress positive and did not give a date.

Fourth, cost discipline. This quarter's operating expenses came in above guidance, and the fourth-quarter G.A.A.P. operating-expense range of one hundred sixty-two million to one hundred sixty-eight million dollars stays high. That is part of why the earnings per share miss and the wider-than-expected adjusted E.B.I.T.D.A. guide weighed on the first reaction, even after a revenue beat and a raised top-line outlook.

Fifth, the end-to-end model: whether launch, spacecraft, and components are truly being pulled through together, and whether new mission classes, including defense-related orbital infrastructure, are becoming real demand or remaining concept-stage.

Edison Yu of Deutsche Bank asked about that more speculative end of the market, including defense-based orbital data centers. Beck said the company has always retained a healthy level of skepticism, but has also been bullish on that category. On a follow-up about what has to happen for the economics to work, he said that for him it is economics, that a number of key things have to happen for those economics to close, and that those key things are extremely difficult. In other words, management is willing to talk about the opportunity, and is not willing to pretend the business case has already closed.

Iridium came up as a financing and process update more than as a new strategic reveal. The company has already funded the pending acquisition through the September at-the-market offering, received lender approval to keep Iridium's credit facility in place, and received overwhelming shareholder approval. Regulatory approval is the remaining advertised gate. Management described progress as positive and did not attach a date. That transaction, if it closes, would add a constellation-operator dimension to a company that has been a builder and a launcher. Until it closes, the four point seven billion dollar liquidity figure already includes the equity that was raised to fund it, and the share count in the fourth-quarter guide already reflects a much larger equity base.

On Neutron timing, the official language remains first launch no earlier than twenty twenty-seven, with first-flight hardware moving to Launch Complex Three. Contemporaneous coverage of the call said composite voids in the first-stage tank required replacement and cryogenic retesting, and that management was not ready to revise a one-three-five early-cadence plan. The seventy million dollar price, already in backlog, is the demand side of that story. The date and the first-flight hardware work are the execution side.

Beck's closing was short. He thanked participants for joining and for the questions, said the company was very pleased with the quarter, and said it looks forward to the next one.

The stock's first move was down, then it recovered. Benzinga said shares were down six point six one percent in after-hours trading Monday, at fifty-four dollars and ninety-seven cents. Investing.com said the stock slumped more than five percent in pre-market Tuesday despite the revenue beat and stronger-than-expected revenue guidance. A later Investing.com note said pre-market was down six point five nine percent, but that the stock had reversed and was trading at sixty dollars and sixty-nine cents, up three point one zero percent, as of twelve thirty-one Eastern on November tenth. The same note pointed out that Rocket Lab had run up nearly eleven percent in the session before the report, so some good news may already have been priced in. TipRanks and others also noted that the fourth-quarter adjusted E.B.I.T.D.A. guide was weaker than consensus, which helped explain the negative after-hours reaction.

Here is what is fair to watch next.

First, fourth-quarter revenue against the two hundred seventy to two hundred ninety million dollar range, and whether gross margins expand as guided, even as the adjusted E.B.I.T.D.A. loss of fourteen to twenty million dollars is wider than some estimates.

Second, Neutron. The first-flight date is now no earlier than twenty twenty-seven, the early backlog is sixteen missions at a higher base price, and the tank and retest work described in coverage is the near-term execution risk.

Third, backlog conversion. A three point five seven billion dollar backlog is only as valuable as the thirty-two point nine percent expected in the next twelve months, plus successful later conversion of Neutron and longer-cycle Space Systems work.

Fourth, Iridium regulatory approval, the last major announced condition after financing and shareholder votes.

Fifth, cost control. Expenses overshot this quarter, and the fourth-quarter operating-expense guide remains elevated. The company has also issued a large amount of equity, including the two point four billion dollar September at-the-market offering, and will have six hundred eighty-one million basic shares in the fourth-quarter share count, including preferred shares.

Sixth, whether positive G.A.A.P. operating cash flow of seven point two million dollars is the start of a pattern, or a milestone-timing quarter, given that non-G.A.A.P. free cash flow was still a use of twenty-three point nine million dollars.

Let me close with a short recap.

Rocket Lab beat on record revenue of about two hundred sixty-eight million dollars, above both its own prior guidance and consensus of roughly two hundred fifty-six million dollars, but missed on a twelve-cent G.A.A.P. loss per share against estimates of about six to seven cents. Backlog jumped fifty-two percent, to about three point five seven billion dollars, after nearly one billion dollars of new launch contracts and four hundred seventy million dollars of Space Systems awards, including eight Neutron missions at a seventy million dollar base price. Fourth-quarter revenue was guided to two hundred seventy million to two hundred ninety million dollars, above consensus, with an adjusted E.B.I.T.D.A. loss of fourteen to twenty million dollars, a bit wider than some estimates. Neutron's debut is now no earlier than twenty twenty-seven, the Iridium deal still needs regulatory approval, and shares fell after hours before recovering the next session. The follow-through is whether the record backlog converts, and whether Neutron and costs stay under control.
