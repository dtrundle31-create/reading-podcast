US Air Force advances restartable hypersonic rocket engine to power high-speed weapons. From Interesting Engineering, by Kaif Shaikh, published November 13th, 2026.

Here's the headline in one sentence: the Air Force Research Laboratory, known as AFRL, has put more money behind a rocket engine called Draper, built by the American propulsion company Ursa Major. The engine is designed for hypersonic applications, and it has already powered two flights of an experimental missile demonstrator. Let's walk through what was announced, what makes this engine different, and just as importantly, what this news does not mean.

First, the money. AFRL has exercised a contract option worth ten point nine million dollars to keep developing the Draper engine. An option, in government contracting terms, is a pre-negotiated extension that the customer can choose to trigger. In other words, the Air Force liked what it saw and decided to keep going. This award builds on a larger, twenty-eight point six million dollar AFRL contract that was announced back in May of 2025. Under that original deal, Ursa Major was tasked with maturing the engine and integrating it into a flight demonstrator.

That demonstrator is called the Affordable Rapid Missile Demonstrator, or ARMD for short. And it uses Draper as its propulsion system. AFRL and Ursa Major first announced a flight demonstration in March of 2026, and they reported that the vehicle reached supersonic speeds. Then, roughly forty-five days later, a follow-on flight took place. That second flight expanded the demonstrator's flight envelope, meaning it pushed into new combinations of speed, altitude, and maneuvering, and it tested how the engine performed and how well it could be controlled during different phases of flight.

Now, here's an important caveat that the article is careful to make. These flights are a real development milestone, but they are not a publicly confirmed hypersonic flight. AFRL described the March demonstration as supersonic. Supersonic means faster than the speed of sound. Hypersonic is a much higher bar, generally considered to be five times the speed of sound or more. So the program's broader objective is to develop propulsion technology for future high-speed weapons and test vehicles, but what has been publicly shown so far is supersonic flight.

So why is the Air Force interested in this particular engine? It comes down to a trade-off that has shaped missile design for decades: solid rockets versus liquid rockets.

Solid rocket motors are the workhorses of most missiles. Their big advantage is that they're simple and ready to go. The fuel is cast into the motor, it sits there, and when you need it, you light it. The downside is that once a solid motor is ignited, it generally burns at a predetermined rate until it's done. You can't really turn it down, and you can't turn it off and back on.

Liquid rocket engines, on the other hand, offer much more control. You can adjust the flow of propellant, which changes the thrust. But many liquid engines use cryogenic propellants, like liquid oxygen, which have to be kept extremely cold. That means specialized temperature-controlled storage and fueling right before launch, which is not very practical for a weapon that might need to sit on a shelf, or under an aircraft wing, ready at a moment's notice.

Draper is designed to get the best of both worlds. According to Ursa Major, the engine produces about four thousand pounds of thrust, and it runs on hydrogen peroxide and kerosene in what the company calls a closed catalyst cycle. The key point is that both of those propellants can be stored at room temperature. The company says they can remain stored for years. That means a vehicle could be prepared for launch without first having to load or condition its fuel, much like a solid rocket.

But unlike a solid rocket, Draper is throttleable, meaning its thrust can be adjusted up or down during flight. And according to the company, it can also restart multiple times. Picture a missile that can burn hard to accelerate, then throttle back or shut down to coast, and then reignite later in its flight to sprint or maneuver. That kind of flexibility gives designers far more control over a vehicle's flight profile than a conventional solid motor allows.

Why does that matter? The article points to two uses. One is hypersonic test vehicles. The military needs targets and test articles that can reproduce the changing speed and maneuverability of advanced threats, the kind of hypersonic weapons that adversaries are developing. An engine that can throttle and restart can mimic those behaviors more realistically. The second use is future tactical weapons that need more control over their propulsion during flight.

The ARMD program itself is also about speed of development, not just speed of flight. It's intended to demonstrate how quickly a new propulsion system and a missile-like vehicle can be developed, integrated, and flown. Ursa Major says the first ARMD flight came about eight months after the contract began. And the second flight, coming about a month and a half after the first, showed a rapid turnaround between missions. In a defense world where programs can take a decade or more, that pace is a big part of the pitch.

Draper is also central to Ursa Major's broader hypersonics business. In February of 2026, the company unveiled a missile system called HAVOC, which it describes as a medium-range hypersonic weapon powered by the Draper engine. The company says HAVOC can throttle and restart during different phases of flight, and that it's designed for potential launch from aircraft, ships, and ground-based systems. The article is careful to note that these are company-stated design capabilities. They are not evidence that the weapon has entered operational service.

Ursa Major has another engine in its stable as well, called Hadley. Hadley is also a liquid rocket engine, and it has powered Stratolaunch's Talon-A2, a reusable hypersonic test vehicle. In April of 2026, the company reported that Hadley had completed ten successful flights, including missions at sustained hypersonic speeds. So the company does have a track record of hypersonic flight, just with a different engine and a different vehicle.

Putting it all together, the latest AFRL funding supports more than a single, isolated engine project. It advances a propulsion approach that Ursa Major hopes to apply across test vehicles and, eventually, real weapons.

But let's close with the reality check the article offers. The ten point nine million dollar award is a development milestone. Neither Ursa Major nor AFRL has publicly disclosed a schedule for fielding an operational, Draper-powered weapon. And this latest announcement does not establish that HAVOC is ready for deployment. What it does show is that the Air Force sees enough promise in a storable, throttleable, restartable rocket engine to keep paying to develop it, after two flights that reached supersonic speed.

That's the story: a room-temperature-fueled rocket engine that can throttle and restart, two supersonic demonstrator flights under its belt, and fresh Air Force funding to push it further toward the hypersonic future.
