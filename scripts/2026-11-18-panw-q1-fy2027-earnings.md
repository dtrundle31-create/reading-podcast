Welcome to this earnings brief for Palo Alto Networks, ticker P-A-N-W. This episode covers the company's fiscal first quarter of twenty twenty-seven, the three months that ended October thirty-first. Palo Alto released results after the close on Wednesday, November eighteenth, twenty twenty-six, and held its call at four thirty Eastern Time. On the call were chairman and chief executive Nikesh Arora and chief financial officer Dipak Golechha. Chief product and technology officer Lee Klarich joined for the questions. This brief is based on the StockAnalysis transcript of that call — the official transcript file on Palo Alto's investor site was not available when this was written — along with the company's earnings release, its Applied Compute acquisition announcement, the related Form eight-K, and consensus figures reported by C-N-B-C from LSEG and by Investing.com. I will keep the numbers short, spend most of the time on what management said and what analysts pressed them about, and close with what to watch next.

Here is the short recap. Revenue was three point four four billion dollars, up thirty-nine percent from a year ago, ahead of the three point three one billion dollars Wall Street expected, according to LSEG figures reported by C-N-B-C, and ahead of the company's own guidance of three point three zero to three point three one billion. Non-G.A.A.P. earnings were one dollar and five cents a share, against a consensus of ninety-seven cents and company guidance of ninety-six to ninety-eight cents. Next-generation security annual recurring revenue, or N.G.S. A.R.R., rose sixty-six percent to nine point seven three billion dollars, which management called one of the largest beats in company history; they had guided to about nine point five five billion. Remaining performance obligations, the backlog of contracted revenue, grew thirty-five percent to twenty-one billion dollars. Management raised its full-year outlook. It now expects revenue of fourteen point five two to fourteen point five seven billion dollars and non-G.A.A.P. earnings of four dollars and twenty-six to four dollars and thirty cents a share, up from fourteen point one zero to fourteen point two zero billion and four sixteen to four nineteen a share, and above the analyst consensus of about four eighteen a share and fourteen point one six billion dollars in revenue. That outlook is for standalone Palo Alto and does not include Applied Compute. Operating-margin and free-cash-flow-margin guidance were left unchanged at twenty-nine point five percent and thirty-eight percent.

Now to how management wanted this quarter heard.

Arora came out sounding confident, and he did not waste time on caveats. "We beat guidance across every metric," he said. N.G.S. A.R.R. was up sixty-six percent, "and we delivered one of the largest NGS ARR beats in our history." Revenue rose thirty-nine percent and remaining performance obligations grew thirty-five percent, "both of which accelerated versus the prior quarter." He wanted that timing noticed. "Delivering that kind of result in a seasonally slower Q1 speaks to the strength of the demand function." The cause, in his telling, is not a one-off hardware refresh. "As AI accelerates cyberattacks, enterprises are moving with urgency to replace disjointed tools with unified platforms." That, he said, "gives us confidence to materially raise our guidance for the year." The beat was not only on the top line. "In Q1, we once again exceeded 30% operating margin, setting a new record for operating profitability while integrating large acquisitions."

He then walked through the three platforms, and he wanted the breadth of the acceleration to be the story, not a single product. Network and A.I. security, the largest business, grew twenty percent, with strength across SASE — that is S-A-S-E — as well as software and hardware firewalls. He pointed to a change in what is actually moving across the network. "Agentic traffic on SASE is up approximately nine times year-over-year as autonomous agents continuously connect to applications, APIs, and enterprise data, all of which require real-time inspection." That surge, he said, is a sign that enterprises are moving A.I. into production, and it has "propelled Prisma AIRS to nearly $170 million in ARR in five quarters since it was generally available, making it the fastest-growing product in the history of Palo Alto Networks." Cortex grew more than fifty percent, led by XSIAM — X-S-I-A-M — and observability. Arora's language here was blunt. "As AI-driven attacks move at machine speed, Band-Aid reactive solutions across multiple data lakes are not a plan." He said XSIAM now ingests more than twenty petabytes of daily telemetry and uses A.I. to cut response times from days to under ten minutes. Idira, the identity platform built around the CyberArk acquisition, grew twenty-three percent on a pro forma basis. The company said it added more than one hundred net new logos from the existing Palo Alto installed base, its best cross-sell quarter since that deal closed.

The through-line he kept returning to is platformization, a word he paused on, almost wryly: "Yes, that word, platformization." Net new platformizations hit a record of about one hundred seventy in the quarter, "more than the last two Q1s combined." Platformized customers now represent two-thirds of next-generation security A.R.R., with net retention above one hundred twenty percent.

Then he turned to the threat environment, and the tone got more urgent. Six months ago, he said, when cyber-capable models like Mythos were first released, Palo Alto predicted that equally powerful offensive A.I. would diffuse through open source within three to six months, "a prediction that has now materialized." He described two parallel effects. The first is a surge in vulnerabilities. "We're seeing critical and severe vulnerabilities discovered nearly seven times the monthly average of last year." He tied recent outages to that pressure. "We believe many of the major disruptions of the last few weeks, including outages across major enterprises and governments, were partially due to unintended consequences of patching and configuration changes, as I.T. teams simply cannot patch these vulnerabilities fast enough without compromising their production environments." The second is A.I.-assisted attacks. He said new agentic frameworks like ARTEX let a handful of operators turn simple instructions into autonomous, multi-stage attacks.

His prescription had three parts, and he did not present any of them as temporary. First, a heavy volume of patches will be required for at least the next eighteen months. Second, virtual patching will grow, letting companies shield infrastructure before they apply the real patch. Third, and "most important" in his words, organizations should modernize "the north of $1 trillion of legacy security and IT infrastructure onto real-time platforms," because "when an attack hits, because it will, defenders have to be ready to detect and remediate in record time." He also wanted to separate Palo Alto's story from the broader A.I. trade. "Every drop in inference cost unlocks thousands of new agentic workflows, putting industry token revenue on a trajectory to surpass $1 trillion by 2029." Then the pivot: "While the market is focused on the AI trade, we are focused on the AI inflection, the shift from building AI infrastructure to real enterprise consumption." Open-weight models, he said, have grown "from under 5% of token traffic to more than 60% over the past year," because enterprises want control of their own data and want models they can train and run on-premises or in a sovereign stack.

That was the setup for Applied Compute, and management treated the deal as the strategic center of the call, not a footnote to a strong quarter.

Arora said Palo Alto had looked across the A.I. ecosystem for a partner with "rare engineering rigor" when it considered training custom open-weight models itself. "We landed on Applied Compute because no other team matched their technical pedigree in post-training open-weight models." The company is bringing a team of frontier A.I. researchers in-house and expects to reach one hundred researchers by year-end. The problem they are trying to solve, as he framed it, is straightforward. "Every enterprise sits on decades of proprietary data, internal playbooks, and institutional judgment that cannot be exposed to public models. But until now, they have lacked a trusted partner to post-train, run, and supervise open-weight models safely on their own." Applied Compute, he said, has already seen "great traction with large enterprises and AI natives alike, achieving north of $100 million in annualized revenue in the last 18 months of founding, putting them in rarefied air."

He laid out two jobs for the business. First, embed A.I. across Palo Alto's own security products. The company, he said, is already training "north of 35 specialized use cases" so customers can "fight AI with AI." Second, "help train and serve secure sovereign implementations of open-weight models to the enterprise." To support that, Palo Alto plans "a diversified global inference capability of north of 100+ megawatts over the next three to five years," backed by what he called "a modest CapEx commitment of less than $3 billion through fiscal 2030, representing approximately half a year of our annual free cash flow." Combined with Palo Alto's go-to-market reach, he said, Applied Compute should "add over north of $1 billion of ARR to our Next-Generation Security ARR by fiscal 2029." He closed his prepared remarks by calling the quarter "a defining inflection point for cybersecurity," and by arguing that A.I. security has to move beyond visibility and enforcement to "ensuring trust in the model itself," because "organizations will be reluctant to put mission-critical workflows on AI until they can rely on how those models behave in production."

Golechha then put terms and financial consequences on that ambition. "The total consideration is $4.5 billion, paid entirely in stock, and we expect to close in our third quarter of fiscal 2027." The acquisition, he said, "includes approximately $500 million in cash and prepaid compute commitments owned by Applied Compute." It will be "top-line growth accretive from day one," and the company expects to scale the business to add one billion dollars in N.G.S. A.R.R. by fiscal twenty twenty-nine. Longer term, he called it "our next multi-billion dollar ARR business." The official deal announcement adds a few details that got less airtime on the call. Closing is subject to customary conditions, including regulatory clearance. Yash Patil, Applied Compute's co-founder and chief executive, is slated to become Palo Alto's co-chief A.I. officer after the close. The Form eight-K says the shares issued at closing will be based on a price of three hundred thirty-seven dollars and sixteen cents, the fifteen-day volume-weighted average closing price through November seventeenth, and that the exact number of shares is not yet known.

Golechha was careful to say Palo Alto intends to stay "capital-light." Compute will first be sourced from cloud partners, with dedicated capacity added over time, mostly in G.P.U.s and servers, and built "in stages against demand that we have visibility into." Cumulative capital spending through fiscal twenty thirty is expected to stay under three billion dollars, "approximately half of our expected free cash flow this year." Then came the margin math analysts would come back to. "Assuming a Q3 2027 close, we expect the transaction to lower our operating margin by up to 100 basis points in fiscal 2027 and up to 200 basis points in fiscal 2028 before returning to our current 29%-30% operating margin in 2029." On free cash flow, he said the company expects to maintain margins of thirty-eight percent or better, "excluding data center CapEx in fiscal 2028 and beyond," and that more precise modeling points will come once the deal closes. He was explicit about one other point: "Our guidance is for standalone Palo Alto Networks, excluding the pending Applied Compute acquisition."

Before the questions, he filled in the rest of the quarter. Current remaining performance obligations were nine point four billion dollars, up thirty-six percent, and contract durations were flat, so the backlog growth was not manufactured by stretching deal terms. Net new N.G.S. A.R.R. more than doubled year over year. Network and A.I. security revenue grew twenty percent to two point three billion dollars. Cortex revenue grew fifty-three percent, or forty percent on a pro forma basis, with XSIAM A.R.R. up more than sixty-five percent to about eight hundred million dollars. Idira grew twenty-three percent pro forma, and he said operating-margin convergence with the core business is running ahead of the original eighteen-to-twenty-four-month plan. Gross margin was seventy-five point two percent, down one hundred seventy basis points, under pressure from higher hardware-component costs and a richer mix of earlier-stage software. Non-G.A.A.P. operating margin still rose fifty basis points, to thirty point seven percent. Adjusted free cash flow was one point nine billion dollars, a fifty-six percent margin.

Then came the questions, and a large share of them were about Applied Compute, about whether the threat spike is durable, and about how all of this changes the company's financial profile.

Saket Kalia of Barclays asked where chief executives and chief information officers will focus modernization spending. Arora said the first rush is into Frontier A.I. Defense, with companies testing their own infrastructure "before bad actors inflict it on them." Many, he said, are "caught in the current sort of crisis of the moment." Customers told the company, "Listen, I cannot manage a fragmented infrastructure." The catch is time: "Whatever they do today is going to take three to six months, maybe nine months, depending on how complex and how tired their environment is."

Brian Essex of J.P. Morgan asked what customer conversations led Palo Alto into sovereign A.I. Arora said visibility and known defenses such as prompt injection were the easy, "deterministic" part. "When the Hugging Face incident happened, it made us realize that we need to build something other than deterministic controls to intercept AI, because deterministic controls cannot anticipate every scenario that an agent is going to conduct, every deception that it is going to try and do." The deeper bet, he said, is that you cannot supervise a model you did not help train. "we have to get in the training business to be able to build a good supervision business. That's our bet." Then he raised the ceiling. "Hopefully this is the right bet for us, and if it works, I think this will dwarf the abilities that we have in cybersecurity. This could be bigger than what we do today."

Rob Owens of Piper Sandler did not get through. Meta Marshall of Morgan Stanley asked whether virtual patching and accelerated patching were themselves causing more outages. Klarich said the opposite: "The virtual patching is actually the solution to the challenges associated with patching too quickly." Organizations trying to move too fast on real patches are what is contributing to recent outages, he said. Virtual patching is a mitigation that is "safe to deploy," and "by the end of this calendar year, we expect that to be tens of thousands" of virtual patches, up from thousands today.

Patrick Colville of Scotiabank asked whether Frontier A.I. Defense is a substantial new category, or "a November 2026 kind of pain point" that levels out. Arora did not call it permanent in its current form, but he also did not treat it as a one-quarter spike. He said model capabilities have not plateaued, "and I do not expect it will plateau for another six to nine months." For "the next 12 months, or pick your number, 12 - 18 months, there is going to be the need for persistent Frontier AI testing." Over about two years, he expects that capability to be absorbed into the platforms, which will "start self-healing."

Shaul Eyal, then at Cowen, asked whether the twenty-eight percent rise in trailing twelve-month firewall bookings was cyclical or structural. Arora reminded him that hardware is "a very small part of our business," and that software firewalls are being driven by A.I. security. Hardware has still picked up, he said, as customers accelerate purchases, as neoclouds harden their infrastructure, and as companies consolidate onto fewer vendors. "We have never seen this level of hardware growth historically. We think this persists, at least for the next 12 months, if not longer."

Fatima Boolani of Citi pressed on a more awkward implication. If Palo Alto starts training and serving models, does it stop being the "Switzerland" of cybersecurity and end up competing with the A.I. partners and customers it sells to today? Arora said the research team will "supercharge and turbocharge" Palo Alto's own products: "If we do nothing else, that's a win." Then he questioned the Switzerland idea itself. "Before you start building a Switzerland product, let's make sure your Apple product works well." In his view, the idea that a security vendor can interpret and stop an A.I. system it cannot see into "is insane. It's not going to happen." He did not really resolve the partner-conflict question, then added, lightly, "Then we'll build LLM too, don't worry."

Jonathan Ho of William Blair asked whether twenty twenty-seven is an inflection for Prisma AIRS as companies move from developer tools to production agents. Arora said it is "very early." If you ask enterprises whether their coding tools are secure, "the answer is going to be, 'What do you mean?' People haven't figured out how to secure Claude Code or Codex or Cursor yet." The first job for Prisma AIRS, he said, is: "don't let your coding agents run amok into your enterprise." Klarich agreed. "The vast majority of other AI and agentic use cases are still very much in the experimentation, piloting, development stage."

Michael Turrin of Wells Fargo asked where the budget is coming from. Arora's answer was unusually blunt. "I think the Frontier AI Defense budgets have just come out of emergency funds." After the events of the prior two weeks, "if somebody has been sleeping under a rock, they must have come out and said, 'Holy shit. If we are not doing this, we better go out and test ourselves.'" He was not worried about money showing up in the short term. His forecast was that cyber budgets will rise over the next two years, "because the spray and pray strategy has not worked in cyber."

Josh Tilton of Wolfe Research asked whether Palo Alto is actually monetizing that nine-times spike in agentic traffic on SASE. Arora said SASE has historically been sold per user, and that "we're going to get more diligent about metering it because, in the end, it increases COGS if you drive more traffic through our SASE fabric." Even at a nine-times spike, organizations have not yet blown through licensed capacity. Traffic monetization, he said, is "still six to nine months out, but it's going to happen."

Gabriela Borges of Goldman Sachs asked him to zoom out on a comment he has made about wanting to be "more in the token flow." Prisma AIRS, he said, "is fundamentally based on inspecting tokens," and could, over the long term, be two to five percent of a customer's token spend. "If you believe there's a trillion dollars of tokens that's going to be sold in the next few years, then by definition, the industry must see a 3%-5% security spend associated with the tokens." Applied Compute fits the same idea. "We are pivoting to ensure all of our businesses are aligned towards token flow," he said, "because over time, as AI gets more and more ubiquitous and as it gets more and more consumptive, we think Palo Alto's business model will align with the consumption of AI."

The last question, from John DiFucci of Guggenheim, named the discomfort some investors will feel. The acquisition, he said, "is so different from what you do today." He asked whether Arora's background in compute should reassure people, and how the financial profile changes if the bet works. Arora joked that he has "made a career out of doing things which I don't have experience in," and that people can "rely on my lack of experience in compute to make me win in compute." Demand can be served on existing cloud contracts, he said. "Doesn't require us to become compute experts." If they later want more margin, they can "get into the risk business of aggregating compute." They are aiming for one hundred to one hundred fifty megawatts, not gigawatts, and only then would he "worry about being a compute specialist." Golechha did not add new numbers. He stuck to a "capital-light approach" and said the company would have "another segment that is very profitable." DiFucci's coda was, "We will see how this goes."

Arora's sign-off was short. He thanked employees, partners, and customers, called it a great start to the fiscal year, and said he hoped they would "continue to work hard to deliver again."

Here is what to watch next.

The first and largest item is Applied Compute. The company expects to close in the fiscal third quarter of twenty twenty-seven, which ends in April, subject to regulatory clearance, including the Hart-Scott-Rodino waiting period. Until then, none of the four-and-a-half-billion-dollar deal is in the official guide. Watch whether the close slips, what modeling points Golechha provides once it is done, and whether a business already at about one hundred million dollars of annualized revenue starts to look like a path toward more than a billion dollars of A.R.R. by fiscal twenty twenty-nine.

Second, the financial shape of that bet. The deal is all stock, so dilution is the price of entry, and the share count in the full-year guide does not include it. Operating margin is expected to be hit by up to one hundred basis points in fiscal twenty twenty-seven and up to two hundred in fiscal twenty twenty-eight, before returning to the high twenties or thirty percent in twenty twenty-nine. Capital spending is supposed to stay under three billion dollars through fiscal twenty thirty, and free-cash-flow margins are supposed to stay at thirty-eight percent or better if you exclude data-center spending from twenty twenty-eight on. Watch whether it stays capital-light, or steps into "the risk business of aggregating compute."

Third, the durability of this threat-driven demand. The beat and the raised guide are built on a shock Arora himself dated in months, not decades: vulnerabilities running at nearly seven times last year's monthly average, and a product, Continuous Frontier A.I. Defense, that he said will be needed for twelve to eighteen months before it is absorbed into the platforms. Colville asked the right question, whether this is a lasting category or a November twenty twenty-six pain point. The next few quarters will show whether virtual patching, platformization, and A.I. security keep compounding after the emergency budgets Arora described have been spent.

Fourth, the ordinary operating questions underneath the deal. Gross margin is already down one hundred seventy basis points, and Golechha said component costs and mix will weigh on it all year. Watch, too, whether Idira margins keep converging as planned while this larger deal is queued up. The second-quarter guide — revenue of three point five three to three point five five billion dollars, N.G.S. A.R.R. of ten point two zero to ten point two two billion, and non-G.A.A.P. earnings of one dollar and one cent to one dollar and three cents — is the first test of whether the raised full-year outlook has real demand behind it. The first-quarter numbers gave management the standing to make the Applied Compute bet. The work of proving it starts now.

That's the Palo Alto Networks brief for the first quarter of fiscal twenty twenty-seven. Thanks for listening.
