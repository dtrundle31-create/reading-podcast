Welcome to this earnings brief for Intuitive Machines, ticker L-U-N-R, often said as Lunar. This episode covers the company's third-quarter results for twenty twenty-six. The quarter ended on September thirtieth. The company published the numbers on Thursday, November twelfth, twenty twenty-six, before the market opened, and held its conference call at eight thirty in the morning, Eastern Time.

A note up front, because it shapes everything that follows. As of the time this script was written, later that same morning, a public transcript of the call was not yet available. I checked the usual places, including Motley Fool's earnings-call transcript pages, and it had not been posted. So this is not a recap of the live question-and-answer session, and I will not invent analyst questions or management answers. The brief is built from the company's press release and related eight-K filing, plus contemporaneous coverage, published consensus estimates, and the early stock reaction.

Here is the headline. Intuitive Machines reported record quarterly revenue of two hundred sixty-nine point three million dollars. The precise figure in the filing is two hundred sixty-nine million, three hundred forty-eight thousand dollars, compared with fifty-two point four million dollars in the same quarter a year earlier, more than a fivefold increase, or about four hundred fourteen percent. The company also reported a much narrower generally accepted accounting principles, or G.A.A.P., net loss, positive adjusted earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization, which I will call adjusted E.B.I.T.D.A., and its first distinctly positive free-cash-flow quarter of the year. Contracted backlog at September thirtieth was one billion, eight hundred sixty-six point eight million dollars, up from two hundred thirteen point one million dollars at the end of twenty twenty-five.

Those results beat the published Wall Street estimates, though the exact consensus numbers differ a little by source. Investing.com and MarketBeat both put the consensus near a ten-cent loss and revenue of about two hundred forty-five point seven to two hundred forty-five point nine million dollars, while a twenty-four seven Wall Street recap used a slightly different consensus, a loss of about eleven cents and revenue of about two hundred forty-seven point six million dollars. Published forecasts clustered around a loss of about ten to eleven cents a share and revenue of roughly two hundred forty-six to two hundred forty-eight million dollars. On either reading, revenue came in clearly above the estimates, and the loss per share was much smaller than expected.

The share count matters when you hear that two-cent loss. Weighted-average shares outstanding in the quarter were about one hundred seventy-three point four million, up from about one hundred seventeen point eight million a year earlier. Net loss attributable to Class A common shareholders was four point two million dollars, or two cents a share, compared with seven point zero million dollars, or six cents a share, a year earlier. The company's overall net loss, before allocating results to noncontrolling interests, was three point four million dollars, compared with ten point zero million dollars a year ago.

Chief Executive Officer Steve Altemus framed the quarter as a proof point, not just a beat. In the press release he said, quote, In the third quarter, we delivered record financial performance across the board. These results are an important proof point in our transformation from a company that advanced the state of lunar exploration into a next-generation space prime developing and integrating the technology, infrastructure and services that enable customers to achieve their differentiated missions across space, end quote.

That transformation shows up clearly in the revenue mix. Product revenue, which the company ties to satellite production, was two hundred fifteen point one million dollars in the third quarter. A year earlier, product revenue was zero. Service revenue was fifty-four point one million dollars, up modestly from fifty-one point zero million dollars. Grant revenue was negligible, at ninety-two thousand dollars. In other words, most of the year-over-year jump is not a sudden boom in the old lunar-services business. It is the satellite-production line, which arrived with the January twenty twenty-six acquisition of Lanteris, the former Maxar satellite factory, and which the company says is now contributing at scale.

There is a useful year-to-date wrinkle in that mix. For the first nine months of twenty twenty-six, total revenue was six hundred sixty-two point two million dollars, compared with one hundred sixty-five point three million dollars a year earlier. Product revenue for those nine months was five hundred twenty-three point four million dollars, again compared with zero the year before. Service revenue, though, was lower year to date, at one hundred thirty-two point nine million dollars versus one hundred sixty-three point eight million dollars. The older services book has not been the engine of this year's growth.

Gross profit, which the company described as record gross margin, was sixty-nine million dollars, or twenty-six percent of revenue. The filing supports that rounding: revenue minus cost of revenues was about sixty-nine point three million dollars, before depreciation and amortization, which the company reports on a separate line. Management said that margin was driven by a larger contribution from the satellite-production business, higher volume, and net favorable one-time program adjustments, including estimate-at-completion adjustments and other cost adjustments. That last point is important. The company itself flags those program adjustments as part of the margin story, so the twenty-six percent figure should not be treated as a proven run rate until later quarters show whether it holds without those one-time items.

Profitability below the gross-profit line is more mixed, and that is where a casual reading of the two-cent loss can mislead. Operating loss actually widened. The company lost thirty-four point two million dollars from operations in the quarter, compared with a fifteen point four million dollar operating loss a year earlier. Total operating expenses were three hundred three point six million dollars, up from sixty-seven point nine million dollars. Cost of revenues was two hundred million dollars. Depreciation and amortization jumped to thirteen point two million dollars from eight hundred fifteen thousand dollars, which is what you would expect after a large manufacturing acquisition. Research and development rose to seven point five million dollars, and general and administrative expense, excluding depreciation and amortization, jumped to eighty-two point nine million dollars from sixteen point three million dollars.

So how does a wider operating loss become a much narrower net loss, and then a positive adjusted E.B.I.T.D.A. figure? The gap is in the adjustments and in other income. Total other income, net, was thirty point nine million dollars in the quarter. Almost all of that came from non-cash fair-value items: a thirty point zero million dollar gain from the change in the fair value of warrant liabilities, and a three point six million dollar gain from the change in the fair value of contingent consideration liabilities. Interest income was three point zero million dollars, interest expense was four point five million dollars, and there was no income-tax expense in the quarter. After those items, net loss was three point four million dollars.

Adjusted E.B.I.T.D.A., a non-G.A.A.P. measure, was positive eight point three million dollars, compared with a loss of thirteen point two million dollars in the year-ago quarter. The company's reconciliation starts from that three point four million dollar net loss and adds back depreciation and amortization of thirteen point two million dollars, interest expense of four point five million dollars, acquisition and integration costs of nine point six million dollars, non-recurring legal expense of ten point three million dollars, and share-based compensation of ten point six million dollars, while subtracting interest income of three point zero million dollars and the warrant and contingent-consideration gains. That math lands at eight point three million dollars of adjusted E.B.I.T.D.A.

Two things are worth holding onto from that bridge. First, the G.A.A.P. net-loss improvement was helped in a major way by those warrant and contingent-consideration gains, which can move around with the share price and with valuation assumptions, and which are not cash from selling satellites or flying missions. Second, adjusted E.B.I.T.D.A. adds back real cash costs the company treats as non-recurring or transactional, including about nine point six million dollars of acquisition and integration costs and about ten point three million dollars of non-recurring legal expense, which the filing does not spell out in any detail. For the first nine months, adjusted E.B.I.T.D.A. was still slightly negative, at three point zero million dollars, compared with a loss of forty-five point one million dollars a year earlier, so the full-year goal of a positive figure still depends on a solid fourth quarter.

Cash flow is the other record the company emphasized, and it deserves the same kind of careful reading. In the third quarter, cash from operations was a positive sixty-six point two million dollars, compared with seven point two million dollars used in the year-ago quarter. Purchases of property and equipment were thirty-two point seven million dollars, which the press release rounded to thirty-three million dollars. That left free cash flow of about thirty-three point five million dollars, which the company rounded to a positive thirty-three million dollars. The company defines free cash flow as operating cash flow minus those equipment purchases.

For the first nine months, the picture is still negative. Operating activities used forty-five point seven million dollars, purchases of property and equipment were sixty-six point six million dollars, and free cash flow was a use of one hundred twelve point three million dollars, compared with a use of thirty-three point zero million dollars in the first nine months of twenty twenty-five. This was a strong cash quarter inside a year that, through September, had still consumed a lot of cash.

The quarterly cash-flow statement also shows why a single good quarter should be handled carefully. Contract liabilities increased by seventy-four point one million dollars, accounts payable and accrued expenses increased by fifty-four point two million dollars, and other liabilities increased by ninety-eight point seven million dollars, all of which helped operating cash. On the other side, contract assets increased by sixty-nine point zero million dollars, other assets increased by seventy-two point nine million dollars, and inventory used another ten point seven million dollars. There was also a provision for credit losses of nine point five million dollars, a non-cash add-back in the operating-cash reconciliation. Working-capital timing was a large part of the quarter's cash generation, alongside the operating result itself.

The balance sheet got much larger, which is what you would expect after buying a satellite factory and a ground-station business. Total assets were two point two four billion dollars at September thirtieth, up from seven hundred fifty-seven point two million dollars at the end of twenty twenty-five. Cash and cash equivalents were four hundred sixty-three point eight million dollars, down from five hundred eighty-two point six million dollars at year-end. Restricted cash was another thirteen point two million dollars, so cash plus restricted cash was four hundred seventy-seven point zero million dollars.

The cash decline from year-end is not a mystery. In the first nine months, the company spent four hundred sixty-four point nine million dollars on acquisitions, net of cash acquired, including seventeen point eight million dollars in the third quarter, and raised five hundred eleven point nine million dollars from securities issuance year to date, including ninety-eight point one million dollars in the third quarter, before transaction costs of thirteen point three million dollars. Long-term debt was little changed, at three hundred thirty-six point nine million dollars, and warrant liabilities were fifty-one point four million dollars.

You can also see the new operating shape of the company on the balance sheet. Inventory was seventy point six million dollars, compared with zero at the end of twenty twenty-five. Contract assets and trade receivables were both far above their year-end levels of about twelve million dollars, at one hundred twenty point three million and one hundred thirteen point five million dollars, respectively. There is a new non-current line for orbital receivables of two hundred eight point two million dollars. Property and equipment, net, rose to three hundred twenty-six point seven million dollars, while intangible assets and goodwill rose to two hundred seventy-four point nine million and four hundred six point seven million dollars. Current contract liabilities were two hundred ninety-three point five million dollars, accounts payable and accrued expenses were one hundred thirty-nine point two million dollars, and a new pension and postretirement-benefits liability of forty-three point zero million dollars likely arrived with an acquired business.

Now to the number the company put at the center of its outlook: backlog. Contracted backlog at September thirtieth was one billion, eight hundred sixty-six point eight million dollars, which the press release rounded to a record one point nine billion dollars, up from two hundred thirteen point one million dollars at the end of twenty twenty-five. The company said the increase of about one point six five billion dollars includes six hundred twelve point eight million dollars of acquired Lanteris backlog, thirty-one point seven million dollars from the Goonhilly acquisition, and one point seven seven billion dollars in new awards, partly offset by six hundred sixty-two point two million dollars of revenue already recognized and by ninety-four point seven million dollars of adjustments, primarily from descoping the remaining Power Propulsion Element contract and a rideshare contract tied to the I M four mission. The backlog is much larger, but it is not a straight climb. Some previously counted work has been cut back.

The new awards, the company said, were driven mainly by a multi-satellite program supporting three commercial satellites, the Space Development Agency's tranche-three tracking layer, the Accelerated Missile Defense Tranche Three constellation, a Jet Propulsion Laboratory instrument program known as Eagle V-S-W-I-R, and Hartford, two I M three hundred series platforms for a national security space application. It also cited new or expanded work on the I M five and I M six missions, the Near Space Network contract, and other awards.

For the third quarter itself, the press release said the company booked four hundred fifty million dollars of new awards and Goonhilly acquired backlog, driven primarily by the A.M.D.T. three constellation, the Eagle V-S-W-I-R work, and the Hartford platforms. That figure includes the acquired Goonhilly backlog, so it is not entirely organic bookings.

Two important awards landed after the quarter closed, in the fourth quarter, and they should not be confused with already booked third-quarter backlog. First, Intuitive Machines said it was awarded as a vendor on NASA's next-generation Commercial Lunar Payload Services two contract, often called C.L.P.S. two. That is an indefinite-delivery, indefinite-quantity contract, or I.D.I.Q., with a ceiling of ten billion dollars and a five billion dollar option, designed to support more capable cargo missions at greater frequency. A ceiling is not the same thing as funded work. Being named a vendor creates a path to future task orders. It does not mean ten billion dollars has been added to backlog or to revenue.

Second, also in the fourth quarter, the company said it contracted with NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory for Falcon Radar, an Earth-observation mission that will study clouds and convection from space. The release does not give a contract value for that award.

The company also closed the Goonhilly Earth Station and Comsat acquisition, which it said expands its space-to-ground data-services network.

On the operating calendar, Altemus pointed to the next missions. He said the company remains laser focused on I M three along with its first Altus-one lunar satellite, which remains on track for a planned launch in the March and April timeframe, and that the emphasis will be to scale execution, accelerate growth, and convert contracted backlog into revenue through repeatable production and reliable customer delivery. The press release also said the company has accelerated plans for the Near Space Network cislunar constellation, with Altus-two through Altus-five to deploy together in twenty twenty-eight on a NASA-provided launch vehicle. I will stay with that wording. The NASA-provided vehicle is what the company attached to the later Altus satellites in twenty twenty-eight. The March and April launch window is what it attached to I M three and Altus-one as it looks toward twenty twenty-seven.

That may also be a shift from what management had said after the second quarter, at least on timing. On the second-quarter call, according to the Motley Fool transcript of that earlier call, Altemus had described Mission Three as scheduled in a January-through-March launch window on a SpaceX Falcon nine. Today's release speaks of a planned launch in the March and April timeframe. Without this morning's transcript, that may be a modest window update, or simply a shorter way of talking about the same plan.

Now, guidance. The company said it expects full-year twenty twenty-six revenue of nine hundred twenty million to nine hundred fifty million dollars, and positive adjusted E.B.I.T.D.A. for the full year. The press release states those figures as the outlook. It does not, in so many words, say the company is raising or lowering prior guidance.

The comparison still matters, and the sources do not all describe it the same way. On the second-quarter call, management had guided to a wider range of nine hundred million to one billion dollars of twenty twenty-six revenue, also with positive full-year adjusted E.B.I.T.D.A. Today's range sits inside that earlier band, with the low end twenty million dollars higher and the high end fifty million dollars lower. In plain terms, the company narrowed the range, lifted the floor, and cut the ceiling. Investing.com described the new outlook as a raise relative to an analyst consensus of nine hundred nineteen point eight million dollars, not as a raise of the company's own prior range, while twenty-four seven Wall Street described the outlook as kept intact, which does not match the wider second-quarter range.

You can also back into what that implies for the fourth quarter, without treating it as a formal fourth-quarter guide, because the company did not give one. Nine-month revenue is already six hundred sixty-two point two million dollars, so the fourth quarter would need about two hundred fifty-eight million dollars to reach the low end of the full-year range, or about two hundred eighty-eight million dollars to reach the high end. The third quarter, at two hundred sixty-nine point three million dollars, sits inside that implied band. The fourth quarter does not require a dramatic further step-up from the third quarter to make the guide, but it also does not leave a lot of room to fall short.

Here is what is fair to watch next.

First, fourth-quarter revenue against that implied band of about two hundred fifty-eight to two hundred eighty-eight million dollars, and whether full-year adjusted E.B.I.T.D.A. actually turns positive after a nine-month loss of three point zero million dollars.

Second, the quality of the margin. Twenty-six percent gross profit is a standout number, but management already said it included net favorable one-time program adjustments, including estimate-at-completion changes. The next couple of quarters will show whether that margin is durable.

Third, cash flow after this quarter's working-capital swing. Free cash flow was positive by about thirty-three million dollars in the quarter and still negative by one hundred twelve point three million dollars year to date, while cash on hand of four hundred sixty-three point eight million dollars is still substantial but lower than at year-end after the acquisition spending.

Fourth, backlog conversion, and the difference between a headline backlog of one point eight seven billion dollars and actual funded work. The company has already taken ninety-four point seven million dollars of descoping adjustments, and it has not, in this release, broken out how much of the remaining backlog is fully funded or how much it expects to turn into revenue this year versus later. Until the transcript or the coming ten-Q provides that, the size of the backlog should be weighed alongside those gaps.

Fifth, the C.L.P.S. two vendor award. The ten-billion-dollar ceiling with a five-billion-dollar option is a large number, and it is central to the company's lunar-infrastructure story. The thing to watch is not the ceiling. It is whether, and when, NASA places actual task orders, and at what value.

Sixth, execution on the missions already on the calendar. I M three and Altus-one are the near-term operational catalysts, with a planned launch in the March and April timeframe as the company turns into twenty twenty-seven, and Altus-two through Altus-five planned to go up together in twenty twenty-eight on a NASA-provided launch vehicle. Mission success, schedule, and cost will matter at least as much as any one quarter's beat.

Seventh, integration. This is now a company with a satellite factory, a growing ground network after Goonhilly and Comsat, a much larger share count, and a heavier balance sheet. The story Altemus is telling is that Intuitive Machines has become a next-generation space prime, not only a lunar-lander company, and the open question is whether production, margins, and cash conversion can keep up.

Intuitive Machines' third quarter was a clear beat on revenue and on the loss per share, with record revenue of two hundred sixty-nine point three million dollars, a G.A.A.P. loss of two cents a share, positive adjusted E.B.I.T.D.A. of eight point three million dollars, positive quarterly free cash flow of about thirty-three million dollars, and backlog of one billion, eight hundred sixty-six point eight million dollars.
