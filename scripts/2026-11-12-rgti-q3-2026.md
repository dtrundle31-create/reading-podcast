Welcome to this earnings brief for Rigetti Computing, ticker R-G-T-I. This episode covers the company's third-quarter results for twenty twenty-six. The quarter ended on September thirtieth. Rigetti released the numbers on Tuesday, November tenth, twenty twenty-six, and held its conference call the same day. On the call were Subodh Kulkarni, president and chief executive officer, and Jeffrey Bertelsen, chief financial officer.

This brief is based on the Motley Fool transcript of that call, the company's press release filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission, and contemporaneous coverage, including published consensus estimates and the stock's reaction. I will cover the results against expectations, the full-year outlook, management's technology points, notable analyst questions, and what to watch next.

Here is the headline. It was a technology-milestone quarter and a revenue-miss quarter. Third-quarter revenue was one point four million dollars, down from one point nine million dollars a year earlier, because of the timing of system deliveries and customer acceptance milestones. That missed Wall Street badly. Published recaps, including TipRanks and Benzinga, put the consensus near three point five two million dollars, which would make the result a miss of roughly sixty percent. I will not pretend a one point four million dollar quarter was close to that estimate. At the same time, revenue for the first nine months was ten point nine million dollars, compared with five point two million dollars in the first nine months of twenty twenty-five, growth of about one hundred ten percent, and already above the seven point one million dollars Rigetti reported for all of twenty twenty-five. This is a lumpy hardware-and-services business, and one quarter's timing can look nothing like the year-to-date trend.

The company maintained full-year twenty twenty-six revenue guidance of approximately twenty million dollars, assuming customer acceptance of the C-DAC system and associated revenue recognition in the fourth quarter. That assumption is the whole ballgame for the guide. If C-DAC acceptance slips, the twenty million dollar year is at risk. If it happens in the fourth quarter, nine-month revenue of ten point nine million dollars implies about nine point one million dollars in the fourth quarter, a huge step-up from one point four million dollars in the third. That is possible in a milestone business, and it is also a concentrated risk.

On earnings, the G.A.A.P. and non-G.A.A.P. figures tell different stories, and investors should keep them apart. G.A.A.P. net loss was three point one million dollars. Diluted G.A.A.P. loss per share was eight cents, which included a twenty-five point three million dollar non-cash gain from the change in fair value of derivative warrant liabilities. Basic G.A.A.P. loss per share was one cent. Non-G.A.A.P. net loss, which excludes stock-based compensation and those fair-value adjustments, was twenty point five million dollars, or six cents a diluted share. Published recaps said the consensus non-G.A.A.P. loss was about five cents, so the adjusted figure modestly missed. Operating loss widened to thirty-three point two million dollars from twenty point five million dollars a year earlier, reflecting higher research and development. Research and development expense was twenty-five point five million dollars, driven by higher salary costs and work toward large-scale fault-tolerant quantum computing. Selling, general, and administrative expense was eight point five million dollars, up from five point nine million dollars. Gross margin was sixty-one percent, up from twenty-one percent a year earlier, though management noted that margins vary with contract mix and hardware-revenue timing. A sixty-one percent margin on one point four million dollars of revenue is not yet a scale story.

The balance sheet remains the cushion. Cash, cash equivalents, and available-for-sale investments were four hundred ninety-seven point one million dollars at quarter-end, with no debt. Capital expenditures were forty-two point six million dollars for the first nine months, primarily for dilution-refrigeration capacity and fabrication equipment. Operating cash flow was a use of forty-nine point one million dollars in the first nine months, compared with forty-three point six million dollars a year earlier. The company also signed a definitive agreement with the U.S. Department of Commerce for up to one hundred million dollars of funding under the CHIPS Act, to accelerate research in miniaturized readout electronics and cryogenic capacity. As a condition of the award, Rigetti issued seven point seven million shares of common stock to the department, a minority noncontrolling equity interest. For context on scale, the company used about three hundred thirty-six point four million weighted-average basic shares in the third quarter, according to the earnings release, so the Commerce issuance is dilutive but not transformative by itself. The more important funding fact is the one Bertelsen confirmed: the CHIPS cash is still ahead of the company, not already in the four hundred ninety-seven point one million dollar cash and investment total. On the call, Quinn Bolton of Needham asked whether that one hundred million dollars of cash is on the balance sheet today. Bertelsen said it is not, and that the company will draw down the funds as costs are incurred.

Kulkarni's prepared remarks were mostly about the technology roadmap, not about the revenue miss. The company said its Cepheus-one-one hundred eight-Q system achieved a median two-qubit gate fidelity of ninety-nine point five percent, meeting a performance milestone for its one-hundred-plus-qubit architecture and, in management's view, further validating a chiplet-based approach. The system is built from twelve interconnected nine-qubit chiplets. It also achieved ninety-nine point six percent median two-qubit fidelity on an eighty-qubit sublattice and ninety-nine point seven percent on a fifty-qubit sublattice. Rigetti said its superconducting systems continuously achieve gate speeds of fifty to seventy nanoseconds, which the company describes as about ten thousand times faster than trapped-ion systems and one hundred times faster than neutral-atom systems. Those comparisons are management claims, and I am reporting them as such.

The next official system target is a three-hundred-plus-qubit machine with about ninety-nine point seven percent median two-qubit gate fidelity and gate speeds below sixty nanoseconds by mid-twenty twenty-eight, with a larger qubit count per chiplet, faster readout, and expanded cryogenic capacity. Beyond that, Rigetti continues to target a path toward about one thousand physical qubits, about ninety-nine point nine percent two-qubit fidelity, and gate speeds below fifty nanoseconds within approximately three years. The company believes that combination of scale, fidelity, and speed could provide a platform for demonstrating quantum advantage. Management also said future one-thousand-plus-qubit systems will require a transition to thirty-six-qubit chiplets, up from the current nine-qubit configuration, and a shift from coaxial cables to superconducting flex cables and eventually fiber optics to overcome wiring limits. Rigetti is collaborating with Riverlane on real-time quantum error correction, which it believes is necessary for practical applications.

Kulkarni also spent time on why the company thinks a modular, chiplet-based architecture is the right scaling path. In the Motley Fool takeaway of the call, he said this approach enables higher performance at a lower cost on the path toward systems with thousands and eventually tens of thousands of qubits. That is a long-dated claim, and it is the reason the ninety-nine point five percent fidelity result on a one hundred eight-qubit, twelve-chiplet machine was treated as a validation event, not only as a laboratory footnote. If chiplets can be linked without wrecking fidelity, the company can grow qubit count by adding modules rather than by yielding a single giant chip. If they cannot, the mid-twenty twenty-eight three-hundred-qubit target and the later one-thousand-qubit target get much harder. Krish Sankar's later question about chiplet connections was aimed at exactly that risk.

The wiring roadmap is part of the same scaling problem. Coaxial cables work at today's qubit counts. Management said a move to superconducting flex cables, and eventually fiber optics, is needed to overcome physical wiring limits as the machines grow. Dilution-refrigerator capacity is the other physical constraint, which is why nine-month capital spending of forty-two point six million dollars went largely to refrigeration and fabrication equipment. This is not a software-margin business yet. It is a company spending tens of millions of dollars a year to build the machinery that might, in a few years, support a system that can demonstrate quantum advantage.

The gross-margin jump to sixty-one percent from twenty-one percent deserves the same caution as the G.A.A.P. profit appearance. On one point four million dollars of revenue, mix and the timing of a single hardware acceptance can move the margin enormously. Management said as much. Investors should not annualize sixty-one percent, just as they should not annualize one point four million dollars of quarterly sales, or a three point one million dollar G.A.A.P. net loss that includes a twenty-five point three million dollar warrant gain.

Now to the question-and-answer session, from the Motley Fool transcript.

Kevin Garrigan of Jefferies asked first, and the discussion went to the quarterly timing and to the C-DAC acceptance that sits under the twenty million dollar year. That is the practical financial question. A one point four million dollar quarter is only a problem for the year if the fourth-quarter milestone does not arrive.

Craig Ellis of B. Riley Securities asked about the one hundred eight-qubit system and the path to much larger machines. Kulkarni said the one hundred eight-qubit system is already deployed, that only a couple of companies are in that neighborhood, and that, based on the company's data, Rigetti believes it can get to one thousand physical qubits and well beyond that. He also discussed the chiplet approach as a way to reach higher performance at lower cost on the path toward thousands, and eventually tens of thousands, of qubits. Ellis congratulated him and said he looked forward to hearing more.

Gary Mobley of StoneX asked about how customers are actually using the systems, including on the public cloud. Kulkarni said Rigetti's system is one of the most reliable, consistently performing systems available on public clouds right now, and that this gives the company a good vantage point on how customers are using the machines, including people who are still trying to understand what quantum computing can and cannot do, and who are working on applications and algorithm development. He said that ecosystem work matters, because the company does not want to arrive near quantum advantage and only then discover that it needs applications.

Krish Sankar of T.D. Cowen asked about the limits of connecting chiplets. Kulkarni said that as of today the company has limitations coming from connecting chiplets, and that it does not see performance deterioration because it is using an interposer. That is a technical answer with a commercial implication: the modular strategy is only as good as the links between chiplets when the qubit count rises from one hundred eight toward three hundred and then one thousand.

Quinn Bolton of Needham asked about the CHIPS Act funding, including how it will be drawn, and Bertelsen confirmed the one hundred million dollars is not on the balance sheet today and will be drawn down as costs are incurred, quarter by quarter. Bolton also asked about government programs more broadly. Kulkarni pointed to expected initiatives not only in the United States and the United Kingdom, but in other countries, and said Rigetti will participate as it makes sense.

Brian Kinstlinger of Alliance Global asked about go-to-market and customer engagement around the larger systems. Tyler Anderson of Craig-Hallum, and analysts from Cantor Fitzgerald, Rosenblatt Securities, and U.S. Bancorp, also asked questions, including about partnership models for specific roadmap milestones and about how the company plans to share more on error correction and application development as it gets closer to quantum advantage. Kulkarni said investors should definitely expect more discussion of error correction, and also of application development, and that a partnership model is part of how some of the later milestones will be pursued.

He was emphatic that application work cannot wait until the hardware is perfect. Thousands of people, he said, are using the systems every day, many of them trying to understand what quantum computing can and cannot do, and looking at applications and algorithm development. In his view, that is a critical role Rigetti needs to play so that, when the company gets closer to quantum advantage, it does not arrive there and only then say it needs applications. Programming a quantum computer, he said, is fundamentally different from programming a classical computer, so more application and algorithm developers need to come into the ecosystem now. That is a reasonable industry point, and it is also not the same thing as a booked revenue backlog. Exploratory cloud usage can be strategically important and still leave a quarter with only one point four million dollars of recognized sales.

The fidelity numbers themselves need a plain-language translation. A median two-qubit gate fidelity of ninety-nine point five percent on the full one hundred eight-qubit system, and slightly higher figures on smaller sublattices, are the company saying that, more often than not, a two-qubit operation is succeeding at a very high rate. Error correction, and eventually logical qubits, are about turning those still-imperfect physical operations into something reliable enough for useful algorithms. The Riverlane collaboration and the later discussion of error correction are aimed at that next step. The mid-twenty twenty-eight target is not only more qubits. It is also a slightly higher fidelity and faster gates, on a larger chiplet, in a bigger cryogenic plant. Missing any one of those vectors would put the later one-thousand-qubit, ninety-nine point nine percent target further out than the roughly three-year window management is using today.

The stock reacted poorly, as a revenue miss of this size would suggest, though the exact print varies by source. Some recaps said the shares fell about four to five percent after the results. A Motley Fool summary line on the transcript page showed a larger decline, around eight and a half percent. I will not pick a single unofficial last price. The direction was down, and the reason was the third-quarter revenue miss and the fact that the full-year guide now leans so heavily on one fourth-quarter acceptance event. In a milestone business, the market often punishes a slipped or delayed quarter even when management keeps the annual guide unchanged, because the guide itself has become a single-event forecast. That is the position Rigetti is in until C-DAC is accepted.

It is also worth pausing on the share issuance tied to the Commerce Department award. Seven point seven million shares is not a controlling stake, and the company described it as a minority noncontrolling interest, but it is still dilution in exchange for access to up to one hundred million dollars of milestone-based funding that has not yet been received. Combined with the lumpy revenue and the widened operating loss, that is the financial trade-off of the quarter: a stronger technology headline, a weaker revenue print, a still-solid cash account, and a new government partner on the cap table.

The year-to-date numbers should sit beside the quarterly miss, not be used to wave it away. Ten point nine million dollars in nine months, already above last year's full-year revenue, means the business has grown. It also means the remaining nine point one million dollars needed to reach a twenty million dollar year is larger than any quarter Rigetti just reported. Only a successful C-DAC acceptance makes that math look ordinary. Without it, the guide would have to be revisited, and I will not invent a fallback number the company did not give.

Here is what is fair to watch next.

First, C-DAC customer acceptance and revenue recognition in the fourth quarter, which is the explicit assumption under the twenty million dollar full-year revenue guide.

Second, whether nine-month revenue of ten point nine million dollars plus a successful fourth-quarter milestone actually gets the company to that guide, or whether timing slips again.

Third, cash use against four hundred ninety-seven point one million dollars of cash and investments, with no debt, after a nine-month operating-cash use of forty-nine point one million dollars and forty-two point six million dollars of capital expenditures.

Fourth, the CHIPS Act drawdown, since the one hundred million dollars is milestone-based and not yet on the balance sheet, and since it came with a seven point seven million share issuance to the Commerce Department.

Fifth, the technology path from a one hundred eight-qubit, ninety-nine point five percent fidelity system today to a three-hundred-plus-qubit target by mid-twenty twenty-eight, including chiplet connections, flex cables, cryogenic capacity, and the Riverlane error-correction work.

Sixth, whether application and algorithm work on the cloud systems starts to look like a customer franchise, or remains mostly exploratory. A reliable public-cloud system is an asset. It becomes a financial asset only when usage, acceptances, and contracts show up as revenue in more than one lumpy quarter.

Seventh, dilution and funding mix: the seven point seven million shares issued to the Commerce Department, the still-undrawn CHIPS award, and the cash burn implied by a thirty-three point two million dollar quarterly operating loss and forty-nine point one million dollars of year-to-date operating cash use. The company can fund its roadmap today. It cannot treat the cash pile as inexhaustible if acceptances slip and spending stays high.

Let me close with a short recap.

Rigetti missed third-quarter revenue badly, at one point four million dollars versus published consensus of about three point five million dollars, because of delivery and acceptance timing, even as nine-month revenue rose one hundred ten percent to ten point nine million dollars. The company kept full-year guidance at about twenty million dollars, but only if the C-DAC system is accepted and recognized in the fourth quarter. The G.A.A.P. loss was small only because of a twenty-five point three million dollar warrant gain. The non-G.A.A.P. loss was twenty point five million dollars, or six cents a share, a slight miss versus a five-cent consensus. Cash was four hundred ninety-seven point one million dollars, with no debt. Management's emphasis was a ninety-nine point five percent two-qubit fidelity milestone on the one hundred eight-qubit Cepheus system and a multi-year roadmap toward three hundred and then about one thousand qubits. Shares fell after the miss. The follow-through is almost entirely about whether C-DAC closes in the fourth quarter, and whether the technical milestones stay on the published calendar. If the system is accepted and the revenue is recognized, this quarter's miss will look like a timing hole in a growth year. If it is not, investors will be left with a strong fidelity headline, a four hundred ninety-seven million dollar cash account, and a full-year revenue number that missed the company's own guide. That is a sharp distinction, and it should be knowable when the fourth-quarter results are reported.

Until then, the right way to hold this report is as a technology update with a delayed sales quarter, not as a sudden change in the long-term roadmap, and not as a quarter that already secured the year. The fidelity milestone is real, the revenue miss is real, and the year still depends on one customer acceptance in the fourth quarter. That is a simple, high-stakes test for a lumpy quantum hardware company, and it is the right place to leave the story until the next earnings report arrives early next year, when that acceptance will either be in the results or it will not. Thanks for listening.
