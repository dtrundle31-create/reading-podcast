Welcome to this earnings brief for Oklo, ticker O-K-L-O, often said as Oklo. This episode covers the company's third-quarter results for twenty twenty-six. The quarter ended on September thirtieth. Oklo released the numbers on Friday, November sixth, twenty twenty-six, and held its conference call the same day. On the call were Sam Doane, senior director of investor relations; Jacob DeWitte, co-founder and chief executive officer; and Craig Bealmear, chief financial officer.

This brief is based on the Motley Fool transcript of that call, the company's published results, and contemporaneous coverage, including consensus estimates and the stock's reaction. I will cover the results against expectations, the cash and capital-spending outlook, management's key points, notable analyst questions, and what to watch next.

Here is the headline. Oklo is still a pre-commercial nuclear company in the early innings of recognized product revenue, but this quarter it beat Wall Street on both sales and the loss per share. Revenue was one point eight six million dollars, compared with none in the same quarter a year earlier. Investing.com put the consensus at one point zero one million dollars. TipRanks used one point zero three million dollars, and MarketWatch rounded the result to one point nine million dollars against estimates of one million dollars. On any of those published estimates, revenue was a clear beat. Earnings per share were a loss of twenty-two cents, beating a consensus loss of twenty-seven cents cited by Investing.com, TipRanks, MarketWatch, and others. Seeking Alpha described it as a beat of four cents against a slightly different comparison; the twenty-two-cent loss versus a twenty-seven-cent consensus is the figure that appears in most recaps.

The beat does not mean the loss is shrinking. Net loss for the quarter widened to forty-one point seven million dollars, or twenty-two cents a share, from twenty-nine point seven million dollars, or twenty cents a share, a year earlier. For the first nine months, net loss was one hundred twenty-three point three million dollars, up from sixty-four point two million dollars a year earlier, and inclusive of one hundred ninety-five point two million dollars in operating losses. Nine-month revenue was three point zero seven million dollars, versus none in the prior-year period. Net interest income of seventy-two point four million dollars over the first nine months helped offset some of those operating losses. In other words, a large cash pile is earning a meaningful amount of interest, and that interest is an important part of why the per-share loss beat estimates even as the operating loss remains large.

The company maintained its full-year twenty twenty-six cash guidance. It still expects cash used in operations of one hundred twenty million to one hundred fifty million dollars, and cash used for property, plant, and equipment of four hundred million to five hundred million dollars. Year-to-date capital spend was already two hundred eighty point nine million dollars, which leaves a sizable fourth-quarter capex range if the company stays inside the official band. Management presented those ranges as intact, not revised.

The balance sheet is the other number investors cannot ignore. As of September thirtieth, Oklo had approximately three point one billion dollars in cash, cash equivalents, and marketable securities. The company said it had raised two point three billion dollars through at-the-market equity offerings during the first three quarters of the year. That is a very large capital raise, and it is the reason the company can fund reactor construction, fuel-fabrication work, and a multi-year project pipeline while quarterly revenue is still measured in the low millions. It is also a reminder that shareholders have already funded a substantial increase in the cash pile.

DeWitte's message was that the company is now visibly building, not only permitting and presenting. The first project, Aurora-Idaho, has moved from reactor-area excavation into vertical structural steel installation. The Department of Energy approved the Preliminary Documented Safety Analysis for Aurora-Idaho. Management said the documented safety analysis itself is not expected until twenty twenty-eight, and that Aurora at the Idaho National Laboratory is aimed at coming online in the twenty twenty-eight to twenty twenty-nine period. Those dates matter, because they place first power years after this quarter's results.

The more finished hardware story in the quarter was Groves-one, the company's test reactor. Management said it was completed in two hundred twenty-nine days, with a total build cost of approximately sixty-five million dollars, plus about ten million dollars for commercially sourced fuel used in operations. In his closing remarks, DeWitte put the all-in figure around seventy-five million dollars and contrasted that with older presentations he recalled, from about fifteen years ago, in which isotope reactors were discussed at three hundred million, four hundred million, or five hundred million dollars before inflation. He called the comparison an indication of how nuclear can be significantly disrupted. That is management's framing, not an independent cost audit, but it was one of the more forceful claims on the call.

Fuel and recycling are being built as a second line of business, not only as support for Oklo's own reactors. At the Aurora Fuel Fabrication Facility, known as A-three-F, equipment installation is underway, and the company is targeting its first full fuel assembly in twenty twenty-seven. The facility is being developed to fabricate and qualify Aurora-Idaho's first core using secured Experimental Breeder Reactor Two material. The Department of Energy selected Oklo for approximately six point eight million dollars to advance recycled metal fuel work with Idaho National Laboratory, including manufacturing methods, test specimens, performance modeling, and preparation for future irradiation testing. A recycling pilot is targeted for commissioning in twenty twenty-seven, to enable fuel recovery and isotope separation. Management also said it is targeting its first contracted isotope delivery after the start of Groves-two construction. Oklo is exploring whether the fabrication platform could also support third-party work, including metallic fuel, high-assay low-enriched uranium, and later fuel forms, with low-enriched uranium described as material between five percent and just under twenty percent enrichment.

On the customer and interconnection side, the pipeline is large and uneven. Management said nine hundred megawatts are currently under active consideration in the P.J.M. twenty twenty-six cluster study. A partnership with Meta is planned for deployment of up to one point two gigawatts at an Ohio campus, supported by customer prepayments for development, with the Ohio P.J.M. process described as taking place over the next twenty-four months. Oklo also announced a manufacturing reservation with U.S. Forged Rings for capacity equivalent to twenty-seven Aurora modules for the period from twenty thirty to twenty thirty-five, meant to secure domestic capacity for large reactor parts. Aurora units are designed to generate between fifteen and seventy-five megawatts of electricity, with potential to expand to higher-output configurations later.

There were also setbacks, and management did not hide them. DeWitte said P.J.M. withdrew one of Oklo's applications for a potential site in Virginia, and that the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission ultimately dismissed Oklo's complaint challenging that withdrawal. He also said commissioning schedules for the Aurora plant are affected by electrical noise management and by calibration requirements for neutron monitoring systems. Those are the kinds of issues that can move a first-of-a-kind schedule even when steel is going up.

The company also said it acquired ARMEC and C.E.I. to internalize manufacturing expertise and reduce future project costs. Combined with the at-the-market equity raises and the reservation for forged parts, the picture is of a company spending now to own more of the future construction stack.

DeWitte opened with a large ambition: to become one of the largest energy producers on earth and beyond, and to make clean power, heat, and critical isotopes abundant and affordable. The path he described is not a single giant plant first. He said the ability to build a number of smaller-scale projects while working toward larger goals gives Oklo repeated chances to iterate and learn from execution, developing people, processes, and suppliers along the way.

He also pointed to regulatory and policy developments that, in his view, support that model. He said the Nuclear Regulatory Commission has finalized a rule that allows applicants to use relevant technical evidence from Department of Energy authorized reactor projects to support commercial licensing. That is a potentially important bridge, because Oklo's early reactors, including Groves-one, have been going through Department of Energy authorization, and the company wants that experience to count later in commercial licensing. He also discussed Department of Energy authorization pathways and specialized nuclear expertise as Oklo advances an integrated isotope production and processing pilot, and he said Congressional action could make funding accessible for eligible nuclear waste management infrastructure, including recycling, alongside private investment. The Motley Fool takeaway of the call cited a Department of Energy figure of more than fifty billion dollars in the Nuclear Waste Fund at fiscal year-end twenty twenty-five as context for possible future recycling infrastructure. That fund figure is a policy backdrop, not Oklo cash.

The construction update on Aurora-Idaho was unusually visual. DeWitte described photographs showing just over seven weeks of progress, from site and foundation preparation in September to administration-building work by early October, with reactor-area excavation complete, safety-related rebar underway, and vertical steel being installed. Next steps, he said, are continued reactor-area construction and equipment installation, along with procurement, including component requests for proposal, and the fuel-fabrication work needed to support start-up. The remaining authorization milestones he listed were completion and approval of the documented safety analysis, a readiness review, and start-up authorization.

On the Virginia interconnection setback, he was explicit that the decision does not affect the Ohio project or the broader announced pipeline, and that Oklo continues to work with P.J.M. on other interconnection applications. He framed site selection as a funnel. You pick sites, evaluate them, pursue them, and often only a few make it. The U.S. Forged Rings reservation, he said, is what planning for a fleet looks like: arranging production capacity today for large components such as vessels, lids, and support structures that multiple deployments will require between twenty thirty and twenty thirty-five.

He also talked about using artificial intelligence to speed internal work, including automating repetitive technical documentation so that legacy document conversion that once took days can now take minutes, with document-control rules encoded in software. The objective, he said, is to reduce the time and cost of technical work and leave people more capacity for engineering problems.

Groves-one, in his telling, is already a completed loop: an open field through construction, authorization, commissioning, and start-up of a nuclear facility, including the hiring, training, and qualification of an operating organization and the writing and testing of procedures. Because Oklo uses a build, own, and operate model, he said the company can retain that experience and apply it to later isotope facilities, reusing and improving operating programs and supplier relationships. Groves-two, he said, would add processing and product-finishing facilities in addition to the reactor and irradiation capability, allowing the company to produce isotopes, separate and purify them, and prepare finished products. The site location and integrated facility scope were still being finalized.

The chief financial officer then tied the finances to that multi-line plan. Through the first nine months, he said, Oklo continued to advance projects across its business units while maintaining a strong balance sheet. The year-to-date cash flow included nine hundred sixty-five point one million dollars of net cash used for purchases of marketable securities related to cash generated from the at-the-market program, a reminder that much of the new capital was invested in securities rather than spent immediately. Capital spend of two hundred eighty point nine million dollars was described as planned investment. He said the company raised capital proactively to build the financial capacity to advance work across all three business lines, and that the model and the acquisition strategy are designed to retain operating experience across power, fuel, and isotopes.

Now to the question-and-answer session, using the Motley Fool transcript.

The call was a long one, with a long list of analysts, including Joseph Osha of Guggenheim, Dimple Gosai of Piper Sandler, George Gianarikas of Canaccord Genuity, Marc Bianchi of T.D. Cowen, Nate Pendleton of Texas Capital Bank, Derek Soderberg of Cantor Fitzgerald, Ryan Pfingst of B. Riley, Jeffrey Campbell of Seaport Research Partners, Christopher Souther of Truist Securities, Seth Basham of Wedbush Securities, Sameer Joshi of H.C. Wainwright, Eric Stine of Craig-Hallum, Drew Scott of Needham, and Brian Lee of Goldman Sachs.

Joseph Osha of Guggenheim opened with questions that led into fuel-facility capabilities and when early services revenue could appear. DeWitte said there is some potential for early services revenue over time, depending on a few variables, which is consistent with the idea that fuel fabrication and related services could show up before a fleet of Aurora plants is running.

Dimple Gosai of Piper Sandler asked about the timing of the documented safety analysis submission, and about whether Aurora, once operating, would sell power commercially under a Department of Energy authorization or need a Nuclear Regulatory Commission license. DeWitte said the preliminary documented safety analysis is the milestone that allows the company to build, which is why construction is as far along as it is, and that the documented safety analysis will happen much closer to construction completion. On power sales, he and the chief financial officer said that under a Department of Energy permit, Oklo can sell power to the labs, which they have seen as the first initial customer for power from the powerhouse, and that some isotope and irradiation services can also be done, to varying degrees, under Department of Energy authorization. A later conversion into Nuclear Regulatory Commission space was described as the ultimate end state, with flexibility along the way, including possible sales to a data-center offtaker under certain use cases.

George Gianarikas of Canaccord Genuity asked about plutonium, specifically the timeline and potential cost structure for receiving government-held surplus. DeWitte said Oklo continues to make progress through Department of Energy programs, that the timeline should become clearer in the next few months, and that, in general, such material is inclined to be provided as is, with no specific fee or cost, after which the cost of fabrication and related work would sit with the company. That is a carefully hedged answer, not a delivery date.

Marc Bianchi of T.D. Cowen asked the question many investors had about Ohio and Meta: is the agreement binding to build the reactor, and if not, when would it get to that point? DeWitte said it is not binding, as is, to build the reactor, and that this is a little bit natural for a project like this, especially one where Meta has leaned in to help drive a new model. What he emphasized was that Meta brought capital at the beginning, reserved a path to scale, and is a partner rather than only a transactional customer. The chief financial officer added that there are some binding elements, such as capital that provides reservation capacity, and milestones for how that capital is used. So the honest summary is mixed: it is not a binding commitment to build the plant, but it is also not a mere memorandum of interest.

A recurring theme across several questions was project timing: when Aurora-Idaho actually comes online, what has to happen between steel installation and a documented safety analysis closer to construction completion, and how much buffer remains in the commissioning schedule after the comments on electrical noise and neutron-monitoring calibration.

Another cluster of questions went to fuel, recycling, and isotopes. Analysts asked how the A-three-F facility could become a revenue platform in its own right, what the Department of Energy recycled-fuel award actually funds, and when the first contracted isotope delivery might occur. Management pointed to a first full fuel assembly in twenty twenty-seven, pilot-facility commissioning in twenty twenty-seven, the start of Groves-two construction as the trigger for that first isotope delivery, and possible third-party fabrication or technical services later. Those are important options, but they are still mostly prospective.

Seth Basham of Wedbush Securities asked directly why the Virginia P.J.M. application was rejected, and whether that reasoning applied to other interconnection requests, including Ohio. DeWitte said Oklo is active on what he believes is nine hundred megawatts of development capacity in Ohio, and that the Virginia decision does not affect Ohio plans. He said novelty was part of the problem: Oklo was bringing in a mix of fuel sources, and the processes did not know how to handle it, so the default response was not helpful. The chief financial officer added that it was one application, rejected for technical reasons, and that the company does not agree with those technical reasons, but has to play the ball where it lies. On the Ohio follow-up, he said the company is working through the P.J.M. process, which he thinks will take place over the next twenty-four months. The contrast is useful. A prepayment-backed campus plan can sit in the same quarter as a lost interconnection fight, and both can be true at once.

Cost and capital questions rounded out the call. Analysts asked about the Groves-one cost as a template for later units, about the four hundred to five hundred million dollar capital-expenditure range, and about how a three point one billion dollar cash position would be deployed. DeWitte's closing comparison of Groves-one's roughly seventy-five million dollar all-in cost with much larger historical isotope-reactor figures was his attempt to answer the cost-curve question in plain language. The official guidance, though, remains the cash-use ranges, not a unit-cost target for commercial Aurora plants.

The stock reacted strongly. MarketWatch said shares were up ten percent at twenty-one dollars and thirteen cents in Friday pre-market trading, after a year-to-date decline it put at seventy-three percent through Thursday's close. A later Yahoo Finance recap said Oklo rose twenty-two point six six percent on Friday, closing at twenty-three dollars and fifty-five cents, as investors both bargain-hunted after a seven-day decline and cheered the earnings beat. Those are two snapshots of the same session, pre-market and close, and both show a sizable bounce. The bounce does not, by itself, change the fact that this remains a long-dated construction and licensing story.

Here is what is fair to watch next.

First, whether full-year cash used in operations stays inside one hundred twenty million to one hundred fifty million dollars, and whether property, plant, and equipment spending stays inside four hundred million to five hundred million dollars after two hundred eighty point nine million dollars already spent through September.

Second, physical progress at Aurora-Idaho: steel installation, major reactor-component installation, and any further comment on commissioning buffers, electrical noise, and neutron-monitoring calibration. The documented safety analysis is not expected until twenty twenty-eight, and first power is aimed at twenty twenty-eight to twenty twenty-nine.

Third, the fuel and isotope timeline: first full fuel assembly in twenty twenty-seven, recycling-pilot commissioning in twenty twenty-seven, the start of Groves-two construction, and the first contracted isotope delivery.

Fourth, interconnection and customer conversion. The Meta Ohio project and the nine hundred megawatts in the P.J.M. cluster study are the constructive pipeline. The Virginia withdrawal is a reminder that not every megawatt in a study becomes a plant.

Fifth, how the company uses a three point one billion dollar cash position, after already raising two point three billion dollars through at-the-market sales this year, and whether later Aurora units can be built at costs that look more like Groves-one than like the much larger historical figures DeWitte cited.

Let me close with a short recap.

Oklo beat published estimates with one point eight six million dollars of third-quarter revenue, against consensus of about one million dollars, and a twenty-two-cent loss per share, against a twenty-seven-cent estimated loss. The G.A.A.P. net loss still widened, to forty-one point seven million dollars in the quarter and one hundred twenty-three point three million dollars year to date, while the company held about three point one billion dollars in cash and investments after two point three billion dollars of at-the-market equity issuance. Full-year guidance was maintained, for one hundred twenty million to one hundred fifty million dollars of operating cash use and four hundred million to five hundred million dollars of capital spending. Management pointed to steel going up at Aurora-Idaho, a completed Groves-one test reactor, and fuel, recycling, and isotope plans aimed at twenty twenty-seven and beyond, while also acknowledging a lost Virginia interconnection fight and commissioning complexities. Shares jumped on Friday. The follow-through is whether construction, licensing, and interconnection stay on the published calendar.

Thanks for listening.
