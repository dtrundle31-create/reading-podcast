Welcome to this earnings brief for NuScale Power, ticker S-M-R. This episode covers the company's third-quarter results for twenty twenty-six. The quarter ended on September thirtieth. NuScale released the numbers on Thursday, November fifth, twenty twenty-six, after the close, and held its conference call that afternoon. On the call were Jennifer Hills, vice president of finance; John Hopkins, president and chief executive officer; and Ramsey Hamady, chief financial officer.

This brief is based on the company's official earnings-call transcript, its press release furnished with the Securities and Exchange Commission, and contemporaneous coverage of the results, including published consensus estimates. I will cover the results against expectations, what management did and did not guide, the company's key points, notable analyst questions from the official transcript, and what to watch next.

Here is the headline. This was not a revenue quarter, and it was not meant to look like one. NuScale reported revenue of two hundred eighty-one thousand dollars for the third quarter, which the company rounded to three-tenths of a million dollars on the call. That compares with eight point two million dollars in the same quarter a year earlier, a decline of about ninety-seven percent. The drop was expected in kind, if not necessarily in size. The year-ago quarter included Front-End Engineering and Design Phase Two work for the RoPower project in Romania, work that was completed in late twenty twenty-five, with no comparable activity this year. Published consensus estimates still looked for more. MarketBeat and TipRanks both put the revenue estimate at three point six six million dollars, so the result missed by about three point four million dollars.

On the bottom line, the company did better than the main published estimates. The loss per share was eleven cents, compared with a loss of one dollar and eighty-five cents a year earlier. MarketBeat and TipRanks put the consensus at a loss of twelve cents, a one-cent beat. Net loss was forty-five point seven million dollars, narrowing from a net loss of five hundred thirty-two point six million dollars in the third quarter of twenty twenty-five. That year-ago comparison is distorted by a huge drop in general and administrative expense, which fell to twenty-seven point nine million dollars from five hundred nineteen point two million dollars. The company did not present this quarter as a sudden burst of operating earnings. It presented it as a step from design work into physical manufacturing, funded by a very large cash pile, while commercial orders are still pending.

The company did not give formal financial guidance. That is an official fact, not an omission I am filling in. Hamady said on the call that NuScale does not provide guidance as a general matter, and that it wants to be able to give the Street guidance on margins and profitability once an original equipment manufacturer agreement is in place. What management did say is that operating expenses should remain fairly constant for now, that capital deployment will continue at a measured and disciplined pace as the company funds first-article fabrication with Doosan Enerbility and advances engineering and licensing readiness ahead of an ENTRA1 original equipment manufacturer agreement, and that it is not communicating any other significant investments publicly at this time.

Let me walk through the financials with that context.

Revenue of two hundred eighty-one thousand dollars produced a gross margin of one hundred thirty-three thousand dollars, compared with two point seven million dollars of gross margin a year earlier. Cost of sales was one hundred forty-eight thousand dollars, down from five point five million dollars. For the first nine months, revenue was nine hundred twenty-one thousand dollars, compared with twenty-nine point seven million dollars a year earlier. With nine-month revenue still under a million dollars, any full-year estimate that assumed a sudden jump in the fourth quarter would have required a very large fourth-quarter number. One later commentary noted that a lingering full-year estimate would have implied about nineteen point five million dollars of fourth-quarter revenue. The company did not guide to that, and I am not going to treat it as management's number.

Operating expenses were sixty-five million dollars, compared with five hundred forty-nine million dollars a year earlier. Research and development was eighteen point zero million dollars, up seven point zero million dollars year over year, mainly because of higher costs to advance the design maturity of NuScale Power Module components. General and administrative expense, as I mentioned, collapsed from the year-ago figure, which had been swollen by items that did not repeat at anything like the same scale. Other expenses rose by eight point one million dollars year over year, the company said, in part because engineers and project personnel were working on fewer commercial projects, so less cost was allocated into cost of sales, and in part because of higher compensation as the company ramped resources for supply-chain readiness. Investment income was nineteen point one million dollars, up from five point eight million dollars, reflecting a stronger cash position and higher yields. That interest income is a meaningful offset to the operating burn, but it is not module revenue.

Liquidity is the financial story investors can actually underwrite today. NuScale ended the quarter with approximately one point nine billion dollars in cash, cash equivalents, and short- and long-term investments, compared with about nine hundred sixteen million dollars a year earlier. One commentary separately noted that cash and equivalents, the portion not placed in investments, came to five hundred thirteen million dollars at September thirtieth. Long-lead material work in process was sixty-nine point seven million dollars, reflecting the continued accumulation of inventory and materials for module fabrication. Hamady said the company has adequate liquidity to fund first-article fabrication with Doosan and to advance engineering and licensing readiness ahead of an ENTRA1 original equipment manufacturer agreement, and that it expects capital deployment to remain measured and disciplined.

Hopkins used the quarter to argue that NuScale has moved from development to physical production. The centerpiece is a contract with Doosan Enerbility for first-article production of the upper reactor coolant system of the NuScale Power Module. Hopkins said the contract moves the company's Nuclear Regulatory Commission-approved design into actual build and sets the foundation for all modules to follow. In the press release he said, quote, This was a significant quarter for NuScale as we progressed from development to physical production of our first commercial NuScale Power Module, end quote. On the call he added that customers and investors need evidence that modules can actually be built to nuclear quality standards and on schedule, and that first articles help provide that evidence and surface early issues before series production.

Around that milestone, management pointed to a supply chain of more than sixty specialized suppliers covering critical scopes such as fuel, major forgings, and module handling cranes. It said it completed first-of-a-kind fabrication for the passive emergency core cooling system with MillenniTEK. It added one new E-two center in the quarter, bringing the global total to thirteen, to support operator training and technology awareness. It also said it has deployed nuclear-specific artificial intelligence tools across engineering and knowledge management to accelerate design workflows.

The product itself has not changed in the headline specifications. Each NuScale Power Module is designed for seventy-seven megawatts of electricity, using standard light-water reactor technology and conventional nuclear fuel available today. The design is certified for plants with up to twelve modules, or nine hundred twenty-four megawatts in total. Management estimated that fifty to sixty percent of prior licensing work is reusable for future domestic construction and operation license applications, often called COLA work. Hopkins also emphasized that NuScale holds what it called the only Nuclear Regulatory Commission-approved small modular reactor design in the world, and that the technology uses proven passive safety features and can be deployed where customers need reliable power.

Hopkins's prepared remarks spent most of their time on readiness rather than on the quarter's tiny revenue line, and that is the case he wants shareholders to hear. He said recent quarters had been about commercial readiness as a technology provider and original equipment manufacturer, and that this quarter took a significant step: the company progressed from development to physical production. Quote, We are no longer simply designing and planning, we are manufacturing, end quote. He described the Doosan agreement as a landmark, and said that while ENTRA1 continues work to finalize arrangements required for project development, NuScale is not standing still. It is strengthening original equipment manufacturer capabilities, deepening supply-chain readiness, and positioning for full-scale production.

His capital message was conservative. The approach, he said, is to stay disciplined on capital, hit the milestones the company can control, and ensure that when commercial agreements are finalized, NuScale can deliver without unnecessary delay or added risk. He said the company is ready with approved technology, proven fuel technology, an engineering and regulatory foundation, strategic partnerships, and a global supply chain. He tied that readiness to a broader demand story: artificial intelligence, data-center growth, industrial energy needs, and energy security, all of which, in his telling, are creating demand for reliable, affordable, carbon-free baseload power.

He also walked through the work that is already underway while ENTRA1 advances customer engagements. NuScale, he said, is advancing detailed engineering, locking in key suppliers, and preparing to support the construction and operating license application process, including for a potential Tennessee Valley Authority project. The licensing team, he said, is already preparing to support that application. He called it familiar ground, because the company holds a Nuclear Regulatory Commission design certification and standard design approvals, and because it completed substantial licensing work on the earlier carbon-free power project. A meaningful portion of that prior effort, he said, is directly reusable for T.V.A. or any other future domestic project, which should compress both schedule and cost relative to a first-time application.

Engineering teams, he added, are drawing on experience from RoPower and other programs. Once an engineering, procurement, and construction contractor is selected, NuScale would support that work under a separate contract covering its proprietary design, keeping the company involved in design integrity while the contractor leads overall project delivery. Further along the timeline, NuScale is also positioning to support operator training, start-up consulting, and fuel-preparation activities in coordination with Framatome. Hopkins was careful to say those are longer-dated opportunities, but that the company is laying the groundwork now, and that many of these steps run in parallel rather than in a single-file sequence.

He also named specific supply-chain moves meant to take long-lead risk off the critical path. Framatome, he said, is completing NuScale fuel design well ahead of customer commitments under a dedicated agreement. Paragon has been awarded a contract to finish production development of safety instrumentation and control systems, with NuScale jointly owning the underlying intellectual property. Across the broader supply base, the company is driving dual or tertiary sourcing on the majority of packages so that no single supplier becomes a constraint. For shareholders, he said, the Doosan investment positions NuScale to execute when orders are secured, with component costs expected to be recovered through the owner's contractual payment schedule under a future original equipment manufacturer agreement.

Hopkins was equally clear that T.V.A. is the immediate priority, but not the only conversation. He said NuScale continues to engage utilities, hyperscalers, and major industrial customers, and that the business model combines ENTRA1's commercial development capabilities with NuScale's approved technology, supply chain, and manufacturing readiness. For shareholders, he said, the opportunity is not simply one project. It is the potential to establish a scalable global original equipment manufacturer business capable of supporting multiple customers and deployments over decades. He also said the company expects to provide further updates as discussions mature into actionable commercial commitments. That last clause is worth holding onto, because the call itself did not convert those discussions into named, dated orders.

The commercial pipeline, though, is still mostly about agreements that have not been signed. The press release said NuScale continues to support its commercial partner, ENTRA1 Energy, in dialogue with the Tennessee Valley Authority on what the company described as the development of the largest commercial nuclear power deployment program in United States history. That is the company's characterization of the opportunity, not a signed, funded order. On the call, Hopkins said the process continues, that the company is optimistic, and that what NuScale can control is building the capacity to execute. He called the move into first article a major milestone in preparation for a project such as T.V.A., because it takes the design into physical hardware and helps derisk the path from engineering to commercial delivery. He also acknowledged that the process has been lengthy.

Hamady later clarified the sequencing, which matters a great deal. A power purchase agreement between ENTRA1 and T.V.A. is not itself the sale of modules. After such a power purchase agreement, the company would expect work on pre-engineering, predelivery services, licensing applications, and other engineering services. The sale of the module would come with an original equipment manufacturer agreement between NuScale and ENTRA1. Until those agreements exist, the one point nine billion dollar cash pile and the Doosan first-article work are the tangible evidence, and the T.V.A. opportunity remains a process.

Romania is the other named project, and it is quieter, not clearer. The year-ago revenue was tied to RoPower front-end work that has ended. Asked for an update, Hopkins said there is a new administration in place, that there have been some public announcements, and that NuScale continues dialogue with both RoPower and Nuclearelectrica, one of the major shareholders. He said he had recently sent a request to visit with the current administration to get insight into where the government is headed. Quote, So at this time, that's all we know, end quote.

Now to the question-and-answer session, from the official transcript.

George Gianarikas of Canaccord Genuity asked for a timeline on a potential commercial agreement with T.V.A. and on other commercial activity, noting that the procurement of long-lead items would seem to indicate some confidence. Hopkins declined to give a date. He said the process continues, that the company is optimistic, and that first-article production is what NuScale can control while it waits. Gianarikas then asked Hamady how much operating-expense growth investors should expect. Hamady said the company does not provide guidance, discussed keeping operating expenses fairly constant, and later in the call the company and coverage noted that operating expenses had been maintained within a plus or minus five percent band for eight straight quarters. Management also said expenses would be expected to grow once NuScale is engaged with a project, with increased spending spread between operating expenses and cost of goods sold.

Nate Pendleton of Texas Capital Bank and Eric Stine of Craig-Hallum asked about commercial and licensing next steps, including what happens once a project is far enough along to support a construction and operating license application. The answers tracked Hopkins's prepared remarks: NuScale would look at revenues from engineering and related services, and a substantial share of prior licensing work, fifty to sixty percent by management's estimate, could be reused. That reusable work is one of the few quantitative claims on the call about how a first commercial project might be cheaper or faster than a clean-sheet application. It is still an estimate, not a signed schedule.

Christopher Souther of Truist Securities asked Hamady to unpack the operating-expense comparison and the role of strategic investors from Korea and Japan. Hamady noted that some of the work with RoPower had wound down, as reflected in revenue, and used the question to talk about the company's Korean and Japanese strategic relationships as part of the manufacturing and commercialization push, including the Doosan work.

Craig Shere of Tuohy Brothers pressed on when the company could give the Street more formal financial guidance. Hamady said NuScale will be able to give guidance soon, and that the objective is to help the Street understand profitability once an original equipment manufacturer agreement is in place. That is the cleanest guidance-about-guidance comment on the call. It also means today's sixty-five million dollars of quarterly operating expenses should not be lightly turned into a forward earnings model for a company that still has almost no product revenue.

Derek Soderberg of Cantor Fitzgerald asked about the path from a power purchase agreement to actual module production. That is the question that produced Hamady's sequencing clarification: a T.V.A. power purchase agreement would be between ENTRA1 and T.V.A., after which NuScale would expect pre-engineering and licensing work, with module sales arriving only with an original equipment manufacturer agreement between NuScale and ENTRA1.

Seth Basham of Wedbush Securities asked first about RoPower, which produced Hopkins's answer about the new Romanian administration, and then about other significant investments needed to move toward full-scale production besides the Doosan work, and the timing of those investments. Hamady's answer was short and clear. Quote, We do not have any other significant investments that we are communicating publicly at this time, end quote.

Hopkins closed by saying that while others remain in early stages of regulatory review, technology development, and fuel qualification, NuScale is advancing toward commercial deployment now. He said the focus is securing customer commitments, achieving manufacturing milestones, converting backlog into revenue, and building a scalable, profitable business. The next chapter, he said, will not be measured simply by milestones achieved, but by revenues generated, projects delivered, and lasting value to shareholders.

On the stock, the immediate read was not a simple punishment for the revenue miss. A TIKR report said NuScale rebounded fourteen percent the following morning after the narrower-than-expected loss, and argued that investors were not trading the stock on quarterly sales. That is one published account of the next-session move, not a closing print I am going to treat as the last word, and later commentary also pointed to lingering uncertainty around the ENTRA1 and T.V.A. process. The fair takeaway is that the market was looking through the near-zero revenue line to the cash position and to whether a commercial agreement is getting closer.

Here is what is fair to watch next.

First, the ENTRA1 and T.V.A. process, and specifically the two agreements that would actually change the financial model: a power purchase agreement between ENTRA1 and T.V.A., and then an original equipment manufacturer agreement between NuScale and ENTRA1. Until those exist, first-article work is preparation, not a substitute for orders.

Second, whether management begins to give formal margin and profitability guidance after an original equipment manufacturer agreement, as Hamady said it wants to do.

Third, operating expenses and cash use. Sixty-five million dollars of quarterly operating expenses against one point nine billion dollars of liquidity is a long runway if expenses stay roughly flat, and a shorter one if a project start causes spending to step up. Investment income of nineteen point one million dollars this quarter is a cushion, not a business model.

Fourth, conversion of manufacturing milestones into recognized revenue. The Doosan first-article contract, the long-lead inventory of sixty-nine point seven million dollars, and the emergency-core-cooling fabrication work are evidence of a supply chain being stood up. They are not yet evidence of a revenue ramp.

Fifth, RoPower and the new Romanian administration. The last meaningful engineering revenue came from that project, and Hopkins's update was that the company is still waiting for clarity.

Sixth, the difference between a certified design and a sold plant. A seventy-seven megawatt module and a nine hundred twenty-four megawatt twelve-module plant are licensed concepts. The investment case now turns on whether a customer signs, funds, and schedules one.

Let me close with a short recap.

NuScale missed published revenue estimates with only two hundred eighty-one thousand dollars of third-quarter sales, down from eight point two million dollars a year earlier after RoPower front-end work ended, but it beat the main consensus on the loss per share, at eleven cents versus a twelve-cent estimated loss. The company held about one point nine billion dollars of liquidity, kept quarterly operating expenses at sixty-five million dollars, and did not issue formal financial guidance. Management's message was that NuScale has entered physical production through a Doosan first-article contract, while the larger commercial prizes, especially the ENTRA1 and T.V.A. opportunity, remain in process. The official transcript makes the sequencing explicit: a power purchase agreement would not itself be a module sale. The thing to watch is whether those agreements arrive, and whether the first-article work stays on schedule until they do.

Thanks for listening.
