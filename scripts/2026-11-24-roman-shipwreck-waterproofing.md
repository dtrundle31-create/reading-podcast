2,200-year-old Roman shipwreck reveals an ingenious waterproofing trick. This story was published by ScienceDaily on November 24, 2026, based on materials provided by the journal Frontiers.

A Roman ship that sank about twenty-two hundred years ago off the coast of what is now Croatia is giving researchers a remarkably clear look at how ancient shipbuilders kept water out of their vessels. The wreck is known as Ilovik-Paržine 1, and it dates to the Roman Republic. In a new study in the journal Frontiers in Materials, scientists from France and Croatia report that they found two distinct waterproofing recipes on the ship. One was made of pine tar, also called pitch. The other combined pine tar with beeswax. Even more striking, microscopic pollen grains trapped in those sticky coatings are helping the team reconstruct where the vessel may have been built, and where it was repaired, more than two millennia ago.

For as long as people have traveled by sea, shipbuilders have had to solve the same problem: how to keep water out, and how to protect a wooden hull from saltwater, from microorganisms, and from marine creatures such as shipworms. Despite the name, shipworms are not worms at all. They are marine mollusks that burrow into wood and can cause extensive structural damage.

And yet, until the mid-twentieth century, archaeological studies of ancient vessels largely concentrated on the wood itself. The protective materials that made those ships seaworthy received comparatively little attention. Waterproofing substances remain an understudied part of ancient maritime technology.

That is the gap this paper is trying to fill. The first author is Dr. Armelle Charrié, an archaeometrist at the Laboratory of Mass Spectrometry of Interactions and Systems in Strasbourg. Quote: "In archaeology little attention is paid to organic waterproofing materials. Yet they are essential for navigation at sea or on rivers and are true witnesses of past naval technologies." She went on: "Studying the coatings, we found two different kinds on this vessel: one made of pine tar, also called pitch, and the other of a mixture of pine tar and beeswax. Analysis of pollen in the coating made it possible to identify the plant taxa present in the immediate environment during the construction or repairs of the ship."

The wreck was discovered in 2016, and both the ship and its cargo have already been the subject of several scientific investigations. A view of the excavation at the bow shows a cargo of logs and amphoras, with archaeologists working near the structure of the bow complex. But this is the first study to combine molecular analysis with the examination of ancient pollen, in order to determine both the composition of the waterproofing materials and the types of vegetation growing nearby when those coatings were produced or applied.

The work brought together specialists from the Department for Underwater Archaeology of the Croatian Conservation Institute and from a program called Adriboats, at the Centre Camille Jullian at Aix-Marseille University in France.

Charrié noted that some regions throughout the Adriatic have particular characteristics that led local populations to develop a specific shipbuilding style. Quote: "Only studies like ours offer an overview into these traditions which bear witness to genuine know-how and diverse traditions."

To uncover the recipes, the researchers examined the physical structure, the molecular composition, and the pollen content of the coatings. One of the tools they used was mass spectrometry, an analytical method that identifies substances and measures their abundance based on the masses of their molecular or atomic components.

The team analyzed ten samples of the ship's protective coating to determine the biological origins of the ingredients. Distinctive chemical signatures characteristic of pine trees appeared throughout the samples. That revealed that the primary waterproofing material was pitch, derived from heated conifer resin or tar.

Pitch is a thick, sticky substance that has been used for centuries to seal wooden vessels and protect them from water. When it is heated, it becomes easier to spread over surfaces and into the gaps between wooden components.

One sample, however, pointed to a different recipe. Ancient shipbuilders had combined the pine tar with beeswax, creating a protective substance known to Greek shipbuilders as zopissa. The addition of beeswax made the coating more flexible, and easier to apply while it was hot. Flexibility would have been a valuable property aboard a wooden ship, whose timbers could shift and flex as they encountered waves and changing conditions at sea.

The stickiness of pitch had another consequence that ancient shipbuilders probably never considered. It could capture tiny airborne pollen grains from the vegetation around it, and then preserve those grains for thousands of years.

Different plants produce pollen with distinctive shapes and structures, which allows scientists to identify the types of vegetation present in an area long after the plants themselves have disappeared. By looking at both the variety and the relative abundance of pollen grains embedded in the ship's coatings, the researchers were able to narrow down the possible locations where the pitch was manufactured, or applied during repairs.

The pollen preserved aboard Ilovik-Paržine 1 pointed to a surprisingly diverse range of landscapes. Some of the plant species were characteristic of Mediterranean and Adriatic coastal areas and valleys, including forests dominated by holly oak and pine. Others were associated with matorral, a type of Mediterranean shrubland that can contain olive and hazel trees.

The discovery of alder and ash pollen suggested vegetation growing near rivers or coastlines, either close to the sea or farther inland. Smaller quantities of fir and beech pollen pointed to mountainous environments. That kind of vegetation is characteristic of the northeastern Adriatic coast, where the mountains of Istria and Dalmatia rise not far from the shoreline.

The distribution and composition of the protective materials also suggest that the vessel likely received four or five distinct batches of waterproofing coatings. The stern, or rear of the ship, and its central section shared the same coating. At the bow, or front of the vessel, the researchers identified three batches that differed from one another. Those variations suggest that the ship may have undergone a series of repairs using materials obtained from different locations around the Mediterranean.

Earlier investigations of the ship's ballast, the heavy material carried aboard to improve its stability, had already identified the ancient city of Brundisium as its likely place of construction. That port is known today as Brindisi, on the southeastern coast of Italy, and it was an important maritime center in the ancient Roman world.

The new pollen evidence suggests that some of the waterproofing coatings were applied in the vicinity of Brundisium. Other layers may have been added along the northeastern Adriatic coast, near the region where the vessel ultimately sank.

The findings show how microscopic biological traces can reveal parts of ancient maritime history that might otherwise stay hidden. Even when waterproofing materials appear chemically identical, the pollen trapped inside them can preserve evidence of separate repair events, and of the environments in which those repairs occurred.

Charrié put the point this way in closing. Quote: "While it seems obvious that ships sailing long distances need repairs, it's simply not easy to demonstrate this. Pollen has been very useful in identifying different coatings where the molecular profiles were identical."

The paper appears in Frontiers in Materials in 2026. Along with Dr. Charrié, the authors are Quentin Couillebault, Igor Miholjek, and Giulia Boetto. The study is titled, "Adhesive coatings in naval archaeology: molecular and palynological investigations on materials from the Roman Republican wreck Ilovik-Paržine 1, Croatia."

So the picture is this. A Roman Republican ship that went down about twenty-two hundred years ago off Croatia carried, in the coatings on its hull, two waterproofing recipes and a hidden record of the landscapes it passed through. One recipe was pine pitch. The other mixed that pitch with beeswax, in a combination Greek shipbuilders knew as zopissa. Pollen trapped in those coatings points to coastal forests, Mediterranean shrubland, river or shoreline vegetation, and even mountain trees. It also helps distinguish four or five separate batches of waterproofing, including work that may have been done near Brindisi, and later work along the northeastern Adriatic, not far from where the ship finally sank. That is the story as ScienceDaily reported it on November 24, 2026, from the Frontiers study.
