Robots to assemble solar panels in two minutes for a 457 megawatt US project. This one comes from Interesting Engineering, written by Muskan Bhatia and published November thirteenth, 2026.

Here is the headline idea. A large solar farm being built in Navarro County, Texas, is turning to robots to put its panels together. The project is called Tormes Solar, and when it's finished it will have a capacity of 457 megawatts. The developer is Matrix Renewables, a Spanish company, and the robotic system comes from a US company called Terabase Energy. Their platform is called Terafab V2.

So what exactly is Terafab V2? The easiest way to picture it is as a small, portable factory that gets set up right on the construction site. Terabase describes it as a modular factory. Instead of crews assembling solar hardware piece by piece out in the field, the parts are brought to this on-site production line, where robots do much of the assembly. And interestingly, the system isn't just hardware. It also helps manage and plan the whole construction project, which we'll come back to in a minute.

The upgraded version, V2, launched this past April. According to Terabase, once it's up and running continuously, it can assemble one unit in about two minutes. At that pace, the company says, it can turn out about twenty megawatts of solar capacity in a single week. The system has been field tested, and right now it's optimized for one specific combination of equipment: First Solar's Series 7 modules, and trackers made by Nextpower.

Let's talk about why anyone would want to do this. Building a utility-scale solar farm is a lot of repetitive, physical labor. It's time consuming, it's expensive, and moving bulky materials around a huge site adds even more cost. Terabase's answer is to bring the production line to the farm and automate it.

Here's how the process works, step by step. First, the raw materials get delivered to the site. That means the solar panels themselves, plus the metal hardware: frames, brackets, mounts, and long steel pieces called tracker torque tubes. Those torque tubes are the backbone of a solar tracker. They're long rotating steel tubes that let the panels tilt and follow the sun across the sky during the day.

Next, the robots go to work. They fasten the photovoltaic modules onto those torque tubes, building up finished assemblies. Then comes quality control. The system uses AI-assisted tools to check that everything has been placed and attached correctly.

After that, autonomous vehicles, which the company calls rovers, pick up the finished assemblies and carry them out across the solar farm to where they'll be installed. And finally, there's still a human step at the end. Workers in the field take the assembled units off the rovers and set them onto tracker supports that have already been fixed into the ground.

One of the selling points is how mobile all of this is. Terabase says one of these modular robotic systems can be set up in roughly four hours. That means it can be deployed quickly, and it can also be picked up and moved to a new spot on the site as construction progresses across what can be a very large piece of land. And you're not limited to one. A single project can run multiple assembly line modules at the same time to increase overall output.

Now, back to that software side. Terabase emphasizes that it isn't just dropping robots onto a field. The platform combines the automation hardware with software for planning, tracking materials, and handling logistics. It also includes a digital replica of the construction site, sometimes called a digital twin. That gives project managers a live view of what's happening, so they can monitor progress and coordinate the work more effectively.

What kind of improvement are we talking about? Terabase claims that compared with conventional methods for installing utility-scale solar, its automated approach can double labor productivity. But the company adds an important caveat: actual results depend on how the project is configured, how fully the equipment is used, and the conditions at the particular site. So, doubling is the company's own claim, and real-world mileage will vary.

There's also some track record here. The first-generation Terafab platform has been available since 2022. Terabase says it's been used to install about 40 megawatts of tracker-mounted solar capacity across several US projects, including sites in Arizona and Texas. Worth putting that in perspective: 40 megawatts total from the first generation, versus a 457 megawatt project now, and a claimed pace of 20 megawatts a week for the new version. This Texas deployment is a significant step up in scale.

Looking ahead, Terabase has two main goals. One is to make the system work with more kinds of solar modules and trackers, beyond the First Solar and Nextpower combination it's tuned for today. The other is to automate that last manual step. Remember, workers still have to lift the assembled units onto the tracker supports in the ground. The company is developing additional robots to handle that final installation step too.

So, to sum it up. A 457 megawatt solar farm in Texas is using Terabase Energy's Terafab V2, a portable robotic factory that assembles solar units on site in about two minutes each, checks them with AI-assisted tools, and sends them out on autonomous rovers for installation. It sets up in about four hours, can be moved around the site, and pairs the robots with planning software and a digital model of the project. The company claims it can produce twenty megawatts a week and double labor productivity, though it notes results depend on the site. Humans still handle the final placement for now, but Terabase is working on robots for that as well. That's the story from Interesting Engineering.
