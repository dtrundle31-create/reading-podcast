Welcome to this earnings brief for Target Corporation, ticker T-G-T. This episode covers Target's third quarter of fiscal twenty twenty-six, the three months that ended October thirty-first. Target released results before the market opened on Wednesday, November eighteenth, twenty twenty-six, and held its conference call at eight in the morning Eastern Time. On the call were chief executive Michael Fiddelke, who is finishing his first year in the top job, chief merchandising officer Cara Sylvester, chief operating officer Lisa Roath, and chief financial officer Jim Lee. This brief is based on the call transcript published by Investing.com, the company's earnings release, and consensus figures reported by Investing.com and MarketBeat. I'll keep the numbers short, spend most of the time on what management said and what analysts pushed on, and close with what to watch next.

Here is the quick recap. Net sales were about twenty-seven point one billion dollars, up a little over seven percent from a year ago, and well ahead of the roughly twenty-six and a half billion dollars Wall Street expected. Earnings were two dollars and sixty-five cents a share, against a consensus of about two dollars and seven cents. That is a big beat, but there is an asterisk: about fifty cents of it came from refunds of tariffs that were struck down, money Target got back this quarter. Even without that, earnings per share rose about twenty-one percent. Comparable sales rose five point six percent, driven by a five percent increase in traffic, which is the number Target cares about most. Digital comparable sales grew about ten percent. Target raised its full-year outlook: it now expects net sales growth of around six percent, a point higher than before, and raised its earnings per share range to ten sixty-five to eleven forty, which includes about two dollars and fourteen cents of tariff refunds so far this year. It also said it plans to spend five to six billion dollars on capital projects next year. Shares opened only modestly higher but climbed through the session, trading up about seven percent.

Now to the part that matters most, which is how management wanted this quarter heard.

Fiddelke opened in a warm, holiday register. "It's hard to believe we're already heading into the holiday season," he said, and he described what he wants Target to be: "a place our guests count on for what they need, but also a place that inspires them with things they didn't know they were looking for." He called that "the ambition behind our strategy, creating the most delightful shopping experience in retail." If that sounds like marketing copy, it is also a deliberate positioning statement. Target is not trying to win on lowest price against Walmart or on speed against Amazon. It is trying to win on discovery and taste at a reasonable price.

He then reminded listeners of the turnaround plan laid out in March: four priorities, which he listed as "leading with merchandising authority, elevating the guest experience, accelerating technology, and strengthening our team and communities." His verdict three quarters in was confident but hedged: "I'm encouraged by the progress we're making and the response from our guests. We have lots of important work still ahead, but I'm increasingly confident in the direction we're headed."

The central message of his remarks was that the growth is broad, not a one-hit wonder. "Importantly, this momentum isn't coming from any one category or initiative," he said. "It's the cumulative impact of changes across our business." And the line that best summarizes the quarter: "Simply put, we're giving our guests more reasons to shop us more often." He was also candid about the monthly pattern. Sales grew in all three months, strongest at back-to-school, but "sales did slow down a bit in October," and then, he said, "we've since seen sales accelerate again in November and are currently in line with our expectations." That is worth noting: management volunteered the October soft patch rather than waiting to be asked.

He also put numbers on the investment side of the story. Target is spending more than a billion dollars of extra operating money this year on store hours, training and technology, and about five billion in capital spending, which has funded thirty-two new stores and more than a hundred and thirty full remodels. And he highlighted Ask Target, the company's A.I. shopping assistant on its app and website, which he said is "still in beta, but we're encouraged by early results."

Cara Sylvester, the merchant, gave the most balanced set of prepared remarks on the call, and arguably the most interesting. She started with what's working. Food and beverage grew nearly seven percent, extending what she called "eight consecutive years of annual sales growth." She tied that directly to the big grocery reset done earlier this year, saying the categories that were changed most, snacks, candy and beverages, have grown nearly ten percent since the reset. Target introduced more than two thousand new food items this quarter, and she said there have been "40 million more transactions containing food items so far this year." The point for investors is that food drives trips, and trips drive everything else in the store.

Beauty grew more than nine percent, with what she called "meaningful market share gains," and Target just opened a new prestige-leaning shop called Target Beauty Studio in more than six hundred stores. Back-to-school and back-to-college delivered mid-single-digit growth, and she offered a nice detail: hydration products, think water bottles, grew nearly forty percent. Her summary line was, "Premium style and design don't have to come at premium prices."

Then came the part you don't often hear from a merchant on an earnings call: a plain admission of failure. On home, she said there is improvement, including a bedding overhaul where more than seventy-five percent of the assortment is brand new and priced on average fifteen percent lower, which produced double-digit comparable growth in its first two weeks. But she immediately cooled expectations: "It's a promising start, but two weeks don't make a turnaround." She called rebuilding home "a multi-year effort."

On apparel she was even more direct. "In apparel, our challenges are more significant," she said. Men's, kids, women's swim and young contemporary are growing, but the problems sit in women's fashion and shoes. "I want to be direct about why," she said. "In women's fashion, we made the assortment and value proposition too complex. We lost focus on some of the foundational pieces our guests count on, and we weren't consistent enough on fit, fabric, and quality." And then: "That's on us, and we're making meaningful changes." Those changes include new leadership in those categories, cutting style counts by about twenty percent, and lowering prices on hundreds of items. She was careful on timing, saying these are "fundamental changes to how we build and run the business, not quick fixes," with early signs expected "this spring." That's a clear signal not to look for an apparel recovery in the holiday quarter.

She closed with the holiday plan, and the dominant word was value. Target is bringing in more than eight thousand new gifts, more than half exclusive, most at twenty dollars or less. She mentioned forty-five dollar cashmere sweaters, ten dollar family pajamas, and the return of the thirty dollar Thanksgiving meal for six. Her framing of the consumer was cautious: "Busy families are watching their budgets and being thoughtful about where they spend, and we see them responding when we give them something they love at a price that feels right." And her definition of value: "value is not simply about the lowest price. It is about finding something that feels special at a price that feels right."

Lisa Roath, the operating chief, talked about consistency, and she used a relatable analogy: your favorite coffee chain or hotel, where you know what you'll get before you walk in. "That's why I remain focused on the consistency of our operations," she said. She reported improving guest satisfaction and Net Promoter Scores, with "even stronger improvements on evenings and weekends, when our stores are naturally busiest." On-shelf availability improved by more than a full percentage point. She said the company's science-led purchasing and replenishment systems now cover "more than 90%" of the assortment, up from about forty percent a year ago. She also laid out just how much physical change the stores have gone through in nine months, a list that included a new baby destination, the grocery reset, Beauty Studio, the Fun 101 hardlines overhaul, and new bedding. On delivery, she said two-thirds of digital volume is now fulfilled through same-day services and next-day delivery reaches nearly three-quarters of the U.S. population. She also mentioned that this year's thirty-two new stores are, in her words, "performing very well," and that Target entered eleven new markets. Her line that captures her tone: "we won't confuse progress with potential."

Jim Lee, the finance chief, kept it tight. "Our business is continuing to deliver healthy top-line growth and even faster underlying growth to our bottom line," he said. Notably, he chose to walk through the results excluding tariff refunds, "to offer a cleaner perspective on our underlying financial performance." That was a smart choice, because it pre-empted the obvious objection that the beat was a one-time windfall. On that basis, he said year to date, sales are up six percent, gross margin dollars up ten percent, and adjusted operating income and earnings per share both up twenty-three percent.

He gave color on Fun 101, Target's name for toys, sports, electronics gadgets and pop culture collectibles, which grew twenty-seven percent. He acknowledged that trading cards, a category booming across the industry, account for "about 40%" of Fun 101's year-to-date growth, but stressed that "even when excluding the benefit from trading cards, Fun 101 is still delivering double-digit growth." Expenses rose about ten percent, which he explained as intentional investment in store hours, training, and new stores, plus higher incentive pay compared with a cut last year. Inventory was up about eight percent, which he said supports expected holiday sales, better in-stocks, and newness. And he previewed capital spending of five to six billion dollars next year, "given the strong returns we're seeing on these investments." He also said Target expects a similar level of share buybacks in the fourth quarter as in the third, consistent with keeping its "middle-A credit ratings."

So what did they emphasize, and what did they avoid? They emphasized traffic, breadth of growth, the store as Target's competitive edge, and honesty about what's broken. They talked very little about tariff refunds beyond Lee's accounting, nothing about competitors by name, and they offered no detailed twenty twenty-seven outlook. That set up most of the questions.

Let's turn to the question-and-answer session.

The first question, from Michael Lasser of U.B.S., went straight at the biggest bear argument: can this momentum last into twenty twenty-seven when Target has to lap things like the trading card surge? He asked, in effect, whether shoppers are coming in to grab trading cards and leaving without buying anything else. Fiddelke embraced the long view. "The goal isn't a quarter of growth or even three quarters of growth," he said. "The goal is sustained profitable growth over time." His main evidence was the breadth of traffic gains: "we're seeing traffic increase in five of our six key categories. The broad-based nature of that strength gives us a ton of confidence." He pointed to food in particular, saying "when we build preference in food, it turns into habits that's sticky over time." Sylvester then unpacked Fun 101 to show it isn't just cards. She said toys accelerated, sports benefited from new fan shops in more than sixteen hundred stores, and the gadget business went "from negative comps to positive comps," helped by things like the store brand's ten-dollar headphones. Notice what Lasser didn't get: a direct answer on how much traffic is new customers versus returning ones. Management answered with categories, not customer cohorts.

Lasser's follow-up was about expenses next year: what comparable sales growth does Target need to get expense leverage? That is a classic, specific modeling question, and it did not get a specific answer. Fiddelke said the company will "chase the returns," and spent most of his answer on how excited he is about new stores and remodels, noting that when Target opens a store in a community that hasn't had one, "we see a huge step-up in share." Lee added that the higher incentive pay inflated this quarter's expenses and repeated the year-to-date flow-through figures, then said, "We have always said that flow-through comes with strong top-line growth." Translation: grow sales fast enough and margins take care of themselves, but no comp threshold was given.

Rupesh Parikh of Oppenheimer asked about agentic commerce, meaning A.I. assistants that shop on a consumer's behalf. Fiddelke was candid that it is small: "we're still early in the journey, and volumes aren't high yet." But he said Target wants "to be at the forefront," working with "tools like ChatGPT and Gemini and Copilot." His argument for why Target should do well there was curation: "we like our odds of standing tall as consumers are looking for the best product at the best value." The one concrete data point was on Ask Target, where he said guests are moving "to purchase at about twice the rate through that technology than we see elsewhere." Parikh's follow-up on Beauty Studio drew enthusiasm but little data; Sylvester said Target introduced ninety new brands and that "It is very, very early."

John Heinbockel of Guggenheim asked about the design organization's ability to fix apparel, and how a new marketing chief, Mark, can help Target get credit for its in-store changes. Fiddelke said there's untapped potential in "the power in our team in stores to tell the Target story," and said "I don't think we've fully tapped all of our potential" there. Sylvester called the in-house design team a "powerhouse design organization" and mentioned Target's first-ever creative director at large, someone she referred to as Isaac, who is mentoring the internal designers.

Corey Tarlowe of Jefferies asked management to rank next year's margin opportunities. Fiddelke politely declined: "I will probably stop shy of rank ordering next year's margin guide." But he gave a useful hint about upside. Target grew operating income strongly this year "without a couple of our highest-margin categories performing like we would want them to," meaning apparel and home. As those recover, he said, "I would expect that to be helpful to the P&L." He also called the Roundel advertising business "a continued good guy on the margin line." Lee listed the same drivers: sales growth, the non-merchandise businesses like Roundel and the Target Plus marketplace, and a mix benefit as apparel and home come back.

Spencer Hanus of Wolfe Research asked about the monthly cadence, especially October's slowdown, and what it means for the holiday quarter. Fiddelke repeated that all three months grew, that October was softer, and that November has accelerated. But he was deliberately cautious: "there's a lot of the quarter in front of us. I feel like we strike that tone, I think, appropriately on every Q3 call." He noted the call came "a week out from the key Thanksgiving holiday." Rather than add more numbers, he handed off to Sylvester and Roath to talk up the holiday season. Sylvester mentioned daily deals that shoppers can preview, a steady stream of new merchandise right up to Christmas, and a gift card for the first hundred guests in line on Black Friday. Roath said stores are staffed and trained, and called this year's store work "the most ambitious transformation agenda we've taken on in more than a decade."

The last question came from Simeon Gutman of Morgan Stanley, who returned to apparel. He pointed out that at the March investor meeting he saw a lot of new apparel, and asked why investors should believe the next round of changes will work. Fiddelke opened by conceding, "We aren't satisfied with the flat growth we've seen in apparel year to date," though he noted home turned to a positive comparable in the third quarter. Sylvester repeated that most apparel categories are growing and the problem is isolated to women's and shoes, and she pointed to what's working even there: faster collaboration drops with brands like Parke and Roller Rabbit, and denim, where she said the store's Universal Thread line "is actually driving strong growth for us." Her key line: "That is what is giving me confidence that we know what we need to fix." But she also set expectations for a long road: "Development cycles, these take time. You don't switch out the entire apparel assortment at once." The next areas of focus are tops, dresses and non-denim bottoms, into twenty twenty-seven.

Gutman also asked why credit card income jumped. Lee's answer was brief: "most of what you're seeing in Q3 is mostly timing," and he suggested looking at it year to date, where he said it's up about seven percent. So don't extrapolate that line.

Fiddelke closed by promising candor going forward. He said Target's ambitions for change were "far, far more than we could do even in one year," with work stretching into "2027 and in some categories, 2028 and beyond." And he previewed how he intends to talk to investors: "Here's what's working, here's what's not, here's where we're at on the journey, here's what we're learning."

So the overall tone was confident but disciplined. Management sounded like a team that knows it has momentum and doesn't want to overpromise. What was dodged? Any comp threshold for expense leverage, any ranking of margin drivers, any breakdown of new versus returning customers, and any real twenty twenty-seven outlook. And no one on the call addressed how much of next year's profit picture depends on tariff refunds disappearing, though Lee's choice to report results excluding them suggests the company knows the comparison is coming.

Now, catalysts and what to watch next.

First, the holiday quarter. November started in line with the plan and accelerated from October, but the call came a week before Thanksgiving, and most of the quarter is still ahead. Watch Black Friday week traffic, the Christmas Morning Magic seasonal reset in early December, and whether the value-heavy gift assortment keeps traffic growing without hurting margins. Holiday reporting from other retailers and monthly government retail sales data will offer early read-throughs before Target reports again.

Second, the fourth-quarter report, typically in early March and likely paired with Target's annual financial community meeting, where the company should give its fiscal twenty twenty-seven outlook. That's where the questions Target sidestepped today get answered: what comparable sales growth it expects, whether expenses grow slower than sales, and what margins look like once tariff refunds are no longer flattering the comparison. The guidance currently excludes any future refunds, so any additional refunds in the fourth quarter would be upside to reported earnings, but they shouldn't be confused with the underlying business.

Third, the trading card comparison. About forty percent of Fun 101's year-to-date growth came from cards. Next year Target laps that. If Fun 101 can keep growing on toys, sports, and gadgets, the bull case holds. If the card boom fades, expect comps to face a real headwind in that category.

Fourth, apparel and home. Management has given you a timeline. Early signs in women's fashion are expected in the spring, with broader improvement over the following quarters. Home is described as a multi-year rebuild, and the bedding reset is the first test. These are Target's highest-margin categories, so they are the main lever for profit upside in twenty twenty-seven. If spring arrives without visible improvement in women's apparel, that's a credibility problem, given how directly Sylvester took ownership this quarter.

Fifth, the investment cycle. Capital spending rises to five to six billion dollars next year, with more remodels and new stores. Watch whether the new stores keep performing well and whether expense growth starts to slow as this year's labor and training investments get lapped. The incentive pay bump that inflated this quarter's expenses should not repeat in the same way.

Sixth, the non-merchandise businesses. Roundel advertising, the Target Circle 360 membership, the Target Plus marketplace, and credit card profit sharing together grew nearly twenty percent. These are high-margin and quietly important. Just remember that Lee said the credit card spike this quarter was mostly timing.

Seventh, A.I. and agentic commerce. Volumes are small today, but management is clearly betting on it. Watch for Ask Target to come out of beta and for any data on sales through partners like ChatGPT or Gemini.

Finally, the stock itself. After a large run over the past year, shares rallied again today. That means expectations are higher going into the holidays, and the bar for the March outlook is higher too. The quality of the beat, excluding tariff refunds, is what will matter as the refunds roll off.

That's the Target brief for the third quarter of fiscal twenty twenty-six. Thanks for listening.
