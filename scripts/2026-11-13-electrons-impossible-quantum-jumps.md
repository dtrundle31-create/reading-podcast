Scientists capture electrons making seemingly impossible quantum jumps. From ScienceDaily, based on materials from Forschungszentrum Jülich, published November thirteenth, twenty twenty-six.

Physicists in Germany have managed to film something that sounds like it shouldn't be possible. Using a new kind of slow-motion electron camera, they watched electrons in graphene leap straight across energy gaps that are normally off limits. Under strong enough pulses of light, some of those leaps happened with a probability of one hundred percent. The researchers say the work gives an unprecedented look at quantum behavior, and could help pave the way for a new generation of ultrafast electronics controlled by light.

Here's an everyday picture of what they saw. Imagine stepping into an elevator and watching the floor display tick from one, to two, to three, and then jump directly to twenty. You never pass through the seventeen floors in between. You just arrive. In daily life that's impossible. In the quantum world, electrons can make jumps that are a lot like it.

These events have a name: Landau-Zener-Majorana transitions. Physicists have long suspected they could become increasingly important in high-speed semiconductor electronics. The problem has been watching exactly how they unfold. They happen far too fast for ordinary imaging.

The new study comes from a team at the University of Regensburg, Philipps-Universität Marburg, and Forschungszentrum Jülich. They tracked these transitions directly in graphene, a material made from a single layer of carbon atoms. The results were published in the journal Nature.

To understand why this matters, it helps to know a little about how electrons behave in solids. In semiconductors and similar materials, electrons are restricted to certain ranges of energy, called energy bands. Between those bands are regions called forbidden energy gaps, where electrons can't ordinarily sit in a stable state. Normally, electrons stay inside the band they're already in.

But when the researchers hit graphene with intense pulses of light, things changed. The light's electric field accelerated the electrons, and some of them switched from a lower band, called the valence band, up to a higher band, called the conduction band. They crossed the gap in between through a quantum mechanical transition.

Capturing that took a new experimental technique, combining advanced laser technology with electron microscopy, so the team could look at electron behavior on timescales far too short for conventional methods.

The first author of the study, Vincent Eggers, put it this way: "If you want to know who has won a race, you have to watch all participants. Likewise, we must observe as many electrons as possible at one time to catch those that undergo a quantum mechanical transition while moving."

The key instrument is something called a photoemission momentum microscope. Despite the name, it doesn't produce an enlarged picture of a surface the way a normal microscope does. Instead, it measures the energies of electrons and how they're moving. From that, researchers build detailed maps of how the electrons are spread across different energy states.

One of the biggest technical hurdles was combining those measurements with extremely powerful light fields that oscillate at incredibly high speeds. The team had to set the electrons in motion and, at the same moment, record how their energies and movements were changing.

Rupert Huber, one of the leaders of the work, said: "Our team worked on this experiment for many years. Now, we can track electrons moving across the entire energy landscape of a solid in real time."

Ulrich Höfer, a collaborator and a pioneer in ultrafast electron spectroscopy, described the thrill of seeing it. "Being able to observe such a fundamental quantum mechanical process during our first measurement campaign was a very special moment for us. It was also made possible by the samples we used."

Those samples were a big part of the story. The experiment depended on exceptionally pure graphene made at Forschungszentrum Jülich. These carbon layers were just one atom thick, and they had to be nearly flawless for the measurements to work.

Stefan Tautz of Forschungszentrum Jülich explained: "These precision experiments require material that is nearly perfect at the atomic level. This ensures that impurities do not obscure the delicate quantum dynamics of the electrons." He added that another important factor was the use of what he called "rotated" graphene, a Jülich specialty, which lets the quantum dynamics show up especially clearly.

That careful engineering let the team separate the delicate quantum effects from noise caused by imperfections in the material.

And what they saw was striking. When the light fields were strong enough, electrons crossed the forbidden gap with a transition probability that reached one hundred percent.

That highlights one of the most surprising features of these Landau-Zener-Majorana transitions. Electrons can move between energy states with very high probability even when a substantial gap separates them. The crucial factor is how fast the electrons approach the gap while being pushed by the electric field. That makes this process fundamentally different from the more familiar idea of quantum tunneling, where a particle slips through a barrier in physical space. Here, the jump happens across the landscape of energy, and speed is what makes it work.

So why does this matter beyond the physics lab?

First, the new imaging technique could help scientists understand what keeps electrons from moving in a controlled, predictable way. The graphene experiments already gave an important clue. The electrons start out moving in an orderly fashion, but their interactions with the material's crystal lattice gradually scramble that motion. Understanding those effects could lead to more precise ways of steering electrons.

That knowledge is especially valuable for future electronics that might use the extremely rapid oscillations of light to direct electrons, instead of relying entirely on electrical voltages. Approaches like that could let engineers manipulate electronic properties at exceptionally high speeds.

The researchers also expect their method to work on many other quantum materials, opening a window onto electron behavior that has been out of reach until now, and possibly revealing new ways to control it.

Giacomo Inzani of the University of Regensburg summed it up: "Our experiment opens a panoramic window into the quantum world. Now, we can follow in real time how electrons move through the energy landscape of a material. And this is just the beginning. Our method can be applied to many other quantum materials, opening up completely new possibilities for controlling their electronic properties selectively with light."

The study, titled "Subcycle videography of Landau-Zener-Majorana transitions in graphene," was led by Vincent Eggers and appears in Nature.
