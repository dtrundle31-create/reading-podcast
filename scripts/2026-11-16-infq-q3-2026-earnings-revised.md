Welcome to this earnings brief for Infleqtion, ticker I-N-F-Q. This episode covers the company's third-quarter results for twenty twenty-six. The quarter ended on September thirtieth. Infleqtion released the numbers on Monday, November sixteenth, twenty twenty-six, after the close, and held its conference call at four thirty in the afternoon Eastern Time. On the call were Marcus Kupferschmidt, head of investor relations and strategic finance; Matt Kinsella, chief executive officer; Pranav Gokhale, chief technology officer and general manager of quantum computing; and Ilan Hart, chief financial officer. This brief is based on the Investing.com transcript of that call, the company's earnings release, the quarterly report on Form ten-Q, and published consensus estimates. I will start with a short recap, then spend most of this episode on what management said and what analysts pressed them on, and close with the catalysts and milestones to watch next.

Here is the short recap. Infleqtion reported third-quarter revenue of nine point one million dollars, up twenty-five percent from a year earlier. The company called it a record quarter and said all of that revenue was organic and entirely from quantum operations. Published Wall Street estimates were looking for more. TipRanks put the consensus at eleven point zero three million dollars. MarketBeat put it at ten point seven five million dollars. Either way, revenue came in below those estimates. The official G.A.A.P. loss was nine cents a share, on a net loss of nineteen point seven million dollars. TipRanks and MarketBeat both compared a figure near eight cents with a consensus loss of five cents, so the per-share loss was also wider than those estimates. The company maintained its full-year twenty twenty-six revenue outlook of at least forty-five point one million dollars, and said a sequential drop in revenue and a gross loss were tied to the timing of two government programs and should reverse in the fourth quarter. Two other figures management wanted investors to hear: remaining performance obligations jumped to fifty-one point two million dollars from twenty-one million dollars in the prior quarter, and the company said it had delivered a key technical milestone ahead of schedule, thirty entangled logical qubits working together in a single computation on its Scale system, spelled S-q-a-l-e, using only eighty physical qubits. Cash at quarter end was five hundred sixty million dollars, with no debt, but losses widened as spending rose.

Now to how management wanted this quarter to be heard. Kinsella opened by giving each of this year's quarters a one-word label. In May, he said, the word for the first quarter was momentum. In August, the second quarter was acceleration. For the third quarter, he said, quote, the word for Q3 is execution, specifically technical execution, end quote. The company had committed to demonstrating thirty logical qubits by the end of twenty twenty-six, and he said it had delivered ahead of schedule. He repeated a line he has used before: quote, logical qubits are the keys to the kingdom for useful quantum computing, end quote. That technical story is what he wanted to lead with. The revenue miss against published estimates, the wider dollar loss, and the gross-margin drop were acknowledged, but they were framed as timing, not as a change in direction.

Customer deployments and leading indicators such as growth in backlog and remaining performance obligations, or R.P.O., were, he said, giving the company increasing confidence that compute revenue will grow strongly in twenty twenty-seven and will represent a meaningfully larger share of total revenue. He later said that growth had accelerated in the third quarter, and that the company expects both backlog and R.P.O. to keep growing in the fourth quarter. Quote, twenty twenty-seven is shaping up to be what I call a target-rich environment, end quote. In the question-and-answer session, he put it even more plainly: quote, I'm feeling really bullish about the way things are shaping up for compute in twenty twenty-seven, end quote. That is the commercial claim sitting underneath the technical milestone. He framed Infleqtion as trying to play in both computing and sensing on one shared neutral-atom platform. Quote, we are the only company in the quantum industry combining this breadth of products on a single platform, giving us multiple paths to commercial value, all neutral atoms, all the time, end quote.

Then he turned the call over to Gokhale for the technical heart of the quarter. Gokhale said the company had completed its flagship roadmap target for twenty twenty-six: thirty entangled logical qubits on the Scale computer, all working together in a single computation, with just eighty physical qubits. That is fewer than three physical qubits per logical qubit, which he said reinforces confidence that future targets can be reached with less hardware than originally anticipated. The company had set two technical targets on the second-quarter call, and he said it exceeded both. It had aimed for fewer than ten physical qubits per logical qubit and ended up with fewer than three, an efficiency he attributed to an A.I.-driven advance that cut the number of physical gates needed for logical entanglement, and to the ability to move atoms to connect distant pairs of qubits. It had also targeted an atom-loss rate below two percent per entangling gate, and he said the company outperformed that target by a factor of two. That matters for Infleqtion's approach, he explained, because the company intentionally converts hard-to-identify errors into atom loss. A missing atom tells the software exactly where a problem occurred.

The signature demonstration, he said, ran an algorithm on thirty logical qubits using about one thousand physical operations, which he called one Kilo-Quop. Circuit-level fidelity after those operations was twenty-one percent, versus a random-guessing success rate of just two hundredths of one percent. He said a paper with the technical details would be released within the next few weeks, and he made a claim about the commercial standing of the result: quote, our demonstration sets the commercial neutral atom record for the largest ever entangled logical state, end quote. The next official milestone is one hundred logical qubits capable of one million reliable operations, or one Mega-Quop, in twenty twenty-eight, and a longer-term roadmap toward one thousand logical qubits in twenty thirty. Through hardware and software co-design, he said, the company now expects to reach Mega-Quop capability with as few as two thousand physical qubits, a significant improvement relative to prior estimates. That system, he said, will integrate continuous atom reloading and repeated error correction, so the machine can replenish atoms and keep correcting errors during long computations. In other words, management is treating this result as a de-risking event, not as the finished machine. Gokhale later made that explicit: this quarter's results used a single round of error correction and error detection, and the next frontier is many rounds.

On the commercial computing side, management pointed to several named items. In Japan, the company's quantum processing unit now powers what they described as the country's first operational full-stack neutral-atom quantum computer, under Japan's Quantum Moonshot program, and Kinsella said Infleqtion was the only foreign quantum partner selected for that program. The company already has a deployed system at the United Kingdom's National Quantum Computing Centre. At the Illinois Quantum and Microelectronics Park, Gokhale said the company is finalizing site readiness as it prepares to deliver a quantum processing unit in twenty twenty-seven. He also described work with Eaton on grid contingency analysis, and said a leading global financial institution, which he did not name, ran tens of thousands of shots of a thirty-logical-qubit circuit on the Scale system in the third quarter. Collaborations were announced with Nvidia, Cisco, and Riverlane. Gokhale said engagement is increasingly shifting to co-development, so that customers can help shape the applications and the systems they will ultimately run on.

Kinsella then turned to sensing, with a focus on moving more of the portfolio into deployable products. The company's Ticker clock, spelled T-i-q-k-e-r, demonstrated sustained operation aboard a U.S. Navy support vessel at sea. NASA expanded a quantum gravity contract by twenty million dollars, bringing the cumulative value to forty million dollars, for work with the Jet Propulsion Laboratory that Kinsella said aims to fly the world's first quantum gravity sensor in space, with work expected to continue through twenty twenty-seven and with the next phase including microgravity testing at the Einstein Elevator in Germany. In October, the United Kingdom government awarded an approximately twelve million dollar contract to advance the company's Quantum Spectrum direction-finding technology toward a deployable product. Conventional antenna arrays, Kinsella said, can be as big as a football field. With Quantum Spectrum, an atom-based antenna can be as small as a sugar cube, and the system receives entirely passively. Quote, we are receiving entirely passively, and that is a huge implication in the battlefield, end quote.

Then Ilan Hart walked through the financials, and this was where management had to explain the ugly parts of the quarter. G.A.A.P. operating loss was thirty-four point five million dollars, compared with six point seven million dollars a year earlier. Non-G.A.A.P. operating loss was twenty-two point seven million dollars, compared with five point nine million dollars. Hart said the increases primarily reflect investment in headcount and technology development, as well as the cost of operating as a public company, and that the G.A.A.P. figure also reflects higher stock-based compensation. On the sequential decline, he was specific. He said revenue and gross margin declined because of the timing of revenue recognition on a government program following an expansion award, as well as lower margins on the final stages of another contract as it concluded. Quote, the magnitude of this impact was specific to Q3, and we expect revenue and gross margin to improve in Q4, end quote. Cash used in operations was sixteen point three million dollars, and capital expenditures were five point two million dollars, primarily for computing headcount and labs. The cash balance was down twenty-two million dollars from the prior quarter and included a twenty-eight million dollar temporary payroll-tax benefit. Hart said the company is maintaining its guidance for a modest uptick in cash burn.

He also gave a brief update on a Department of Commerce transaction announced in the second quarter, of up to one hundred million dollars. The company expects to complete the definitive agreement process later this year, he said, and under the current structure the amount is expected to be recorded as a financing transaction, with final treatment depending on the definitive agreement and the accounting review. The deal is not closed, and management did not describe it as revenue. Kinsella closed the prepared remarks by tying the technical milestone to a longer-term financial claim. As computing systems begin solving commercially relevant problems and the sensing business grows, he said, the company expects revenue to grow faster than operating costs, and that is how it will build operating leverage over time. That is a forward-looking statement, not a result this quarter. This quarter, costs and losses widened by much more than revenue grew.

Now to the question-and-answer session, which is where analysts' concerns came through most clearly.

Jesse Sobelson of B.T.I.G. started with the commercial model. Peers are diverging, he said, some selling on-premises systems into national labs, others monetizing cloud access and application partnerships. With systems now operational in the United Kingdom and Japan, how does Infleqtion see the mix evolving between full-stack system deliveries and cloud or software access? Kinsella answered with a note of humility. Quote, we have to have a little bit of humility when we predict the future here, end quote. If you had asked him that question five years ago, he said, he would have thought that by twenty twenty-six the vast majority of commercial quantum activity would be taking place in the cloud. Quote, in reality, it has been the exact opposite. The market is almost entirely driven by system sales, end quote. His current mental model is that system sales will be a big part of the mix for a while, with cloud rising as a percentage over time, and that customers will want machines on their premises early on before shifting more toward cloud consumption.

Sobelson's follow-up produced one of the call's most important numbers. He asked whether the company would disclose the new R.P.O. figure, after last quarter's twenty-one million dollars. Hart said yes, it would be in the ten-Q, and that overall R.P.O. was fifty-one point two million dollars. Sobelson then asked for a split between sensing and computing, and that is where management stopped short. Kinsella said there had been growth in both, that compute had been particularly strong, and that the company also expects good growth in R.P.O. in the fourth quarter. He did not give a dollar split. So the headline backlog number was disclosed. The mix underneath it was not.

Craig Ellis of B. Riley Securities pressed on the technical leap from thirty logical qubits to one hundred. What had the company learned, and what incremental engineering work still has to be done? Gokhale said that, in many ways, getting to thirty validated the key building blocks needed for one hundred as well, notably the ability to pick up qubits and move them around during a computation, which is why the physical-to-logical ratio is so efficient, and the ability to maintain good performance of the underlying physical gates. To get to one hundred, he said, two things still have to happen. The company will be running much bigger computations, so it will need error-correction codes with a greater distance than the distance-four results already shown, and it will need to reload atoms as they are lost. He said a lot of systems-engineering work remains to bring that together into one demonstration. Ellis then asked how customer conversations had evolved, and noted that the company was still not disclosing how much of the R.P.O. is compute. Kinsella said the company had already started to invest more in compute in the third quarter, with more to come, because conversations had increased globally, not just in the United States, and were becoming more specific about applications. Quote, I think you should look to the global markets for where a lot of this, or some of this, strength is coming from, end quote. He still did not quantify the compute share.

Adrienne Colby, asking for Atif Malik at Citi, wanted help modeling twenty twenty-seven. What is the timing of the Illinois park delivery, and how should investors think about the mix of compute versus sensing next year, given the Innovate U.K. contract and the NASA extension? Kinsella was more concrete here than he had been on the R.P.O. split. The plan, he said, is to deliver the system to the Illinois Quantum and Microelectronics Park by the end of twenty twenty-seven, and the company will book that revenue in twenty twenty-seven. For modeling purposes, he said, you can book that ten million dollars as revenue next year, and that this will start to take compute up as a percentage of revenue in twenty twenty-seven compared with twenty twenty-six. Hart agreed. They still did not give a twenty twenty-seven revenue total, a mix percentage, or formal guidance beyond that ten million dollar modeling input. On a follow-up about where the company needs to invest, Kinsella pointed to manufacturing. In addition to ramping compute research and development, he said, Infleqtion needs to be able to manufacture not only sensing products but computers in greater numbers than in the past. He said he believes the company is ahead of the curve because of its sensing business, but that this is something it needs to beef up, particularly if it is going to sell computers in big numbers.

Ella Dolchinsky of Morgan Stanley followed up on the shift to co-development. Gokhale said a lot of recent interest has come from buyers realizing that the cost of running something on a quantum computer can be one hundred or one thousand times lower than their initial estimates, thanks to the reconfigurability of neutral atoms, and that some of the earliest uses are expected to be in materials, better batteries, better solar cells, and better catalysts.

John McPeake of Rosenblatt Securities asked whether a forthcoming technical paper would include a two-qubit gate error rate. Gokhale's answer was careful. He said he thinks it will be in there, at least calculations that back into it, but he explained why the company prefers to measure success at the level of the entire circuit. Running about one thousand operations at a net fidelity of twenty-one percent, he said, implies a per-gate success rate of about ninety-nine point eight five percent, averaged across many types of gates, not just two-qubit gates. McPeake then asked whether the Mega-Quop machine would support universal gates. Gokhale said yes: quote, this Mega-Quop one hundred logical qubit machine will be universal quantum computation, every gate that you can possibly think of, we could do, end quote.

Tyler Anderson of Craig-Hallum, asking for Richard Shannon, wanted to know whether contract manufacturers were a priority for the one-hundred-logical-qubit machine or for a later machine. Kinsella declined to lock in a timeline for computers. He said the company is looking at potential contract manufacturers first for some of its sensing products. As for the computers, quote, we'll have to make that decision, and that's part of what we're looking to bring that kind of D.N.A. into the company to help us make that decision, when it makes sense to engage contract manufacturers, if it makes sense to engage them on the one hundred logical qubit machine or to wait until further machines. I'd say stay tuned, end quote.

Kingsley Crane of Canaccord Genuity asked whether Intel's acquisition of Classiq had changed customer conversations. Gokhale called the deal very validating of Infleqtion's own decision to combine hardware and software, after it acquired Super.tech, the software company he co-founded, in twenty twenty-two, and said the bulk of the company's software attention is now on deep co-design for neutral atoms, on its Superstat platform, spelled Superstaq. Crane also asked whether political crosscurrents and the recent election had changed the tenor or timeline of government awards, including the Department of Commerce transaction. Kinsella's answer was short. Quote, no, Kingsley, the answer, yeah, no, there isn’t. Things are moving forward, and we are getting closer and closer to the finish line to getting that deal inked, end quote.

Quinn Bolton of Needham and Company closed with a technical exchange that is worth translating into plain language, because it is the clearest statement of what this quarter's milestone is, and what it is not. Bolton asked whether a factory-level fidelity of ninety-nine point eight five percent implied a logical error rate of roughly ten to the minus three, and whether the Mega-Quop machine would need something like ten to the minus six to do a million operations. Gokhale said that was effectively the right way to think about it, with the qualifier that the analysis slightly mixes physical-qubit fidelity and logical-qubit fidelity. He then said that getting from ten to the minus three to ten to the minus six is not a one-thousand-times-harder problem, because quantum error correction becomes more powerful as physical qubits are added. Quote, we really think of this as the important zero to one moment happened, and now it's a one to n moment. Still work to be done, but that's why we're feeling good about twenty twenty-eight with one million operations, ten negative six error rates, end quote. Bolton's follow-up went to the heart of the remaining risk. When the company said the system did about one thousand operations, was it actually doing a thousand rounds of live error correction? Gokhale did not dodge this one. Quote, these results are with a single round of error correction and error detection. The next step towards one hundred logical qubits and one Mega-Quop is to do many rounds of error correction, end quote. He said a lot of the key building blocks have been de-risked, but he agreed that multiple rounds are what the twenty twenty-eight machine will require. The milestone is real. It is not yet the multi-round, long-duration machine management is promising two years from now.

Kinsella's closing remarks were brief. He thanked the audience and added, quote, we've got a lot to deliver in the coming months, so we're going to focus on execution, end quote.

Here is what is fair to watch next.

The first test is whether the fourth quarter actually delivers the reversal Hart described. The math implied by the maintained full-year outlook of at least forty-five point one million dollars, and the thirty-two point six million dollars already recorded in the first nine months, is a fourth quarter of about twelve point five million dollars. Management also said gross margin should improve after that timing-driven gross loss. If the fourth quarter does not restore both, the timing explanation will be harder to maintain, and the full-year outlook will be at risk.

The second test is whether the backlog keeps growing, and whether it converts. Kinsella said he expects remaining performance obligations to continue growing in the fourth quarter. Of the current fifty-one point two million dollars, thirty-six point one million dollars is slated for twenty twenty-seven. Those are contracted amounts, not recognized revenue, and management still has not said how much is computing versus sensing. The ten-Q also shows a concentration issue: one customer, labeled Customer A, accounted for eighty-one percent of third-quarter revenue and eighty percent of accounts receivable, and government customers accounted for eighty-nine percent of the quarter's revenue. A rising R.P.O. balance is a constructive signal if compute is a large and growing part of it, and a less comforting one if a single customer or a handful of government programs are doing most of the work.

The third test is the computing mix in twenty twenty-seven. Kinsella pointed to about ten million dollars of revenue from the Illinois park system as a modeling input, with delivery planned by the end of twenty twenty-seven, and said compute should become a meaningfully larger share of revenue next year. Watch whether that system stays on schedule, whether additional system sales show up behind it, and whether manufacturing capacity keeps up. Sensing work also runs through twenty twenty-seven, including the NASA and United Kingdom Quantum Spectrum awards, so a larger compute share is not automatic. Watch, too, for any decision on contract manufacturers, which Kinsella said the company is exploring first for sensing products.

Further out, the official technical target remains one hundred logical qubits and one Mega-Quop in twenty twenty-eight, with a paper on the thirty-logical-qubit demonstration due in the next few weeks. The work between here and that machine, by Gokhale's own account, is multiple rounds of error correction, atom reloading, and higher-distance error-correction codes. Also watch the Department of Commerce transaction of up to one hundred million dollars, still expected later this year as financing, not revenue. Cash remains large, with no debt, but includes a twenty-eight million dollar temporary payroll-tax benefit, and losses and spending are rising. Kinsella's leverage thesis is that revenue will grow faster than operating costs, and this quarter does not yet show it. Upcoming events include Piper Sandler's virtual Quantum Day, Nvidia's G.T.C. in Washington, the U.B.S. Technology Conference, Q two B in Chicago, and a virtual Craig-Hallum quantum event.
