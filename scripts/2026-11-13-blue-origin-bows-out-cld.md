Blue Origin bows out of NASA commercial space station competition. This story was published by Jeff Foust at SpaceNews on November 13, 2026. The original SpaceNews article was blocked behind a registration wall, so this episode is based on similar coverage from Aviation Week and GeekWire, which reported the same decision and quoted Blue Origin's statement.

Blue Origin will not bid in the next round of NASA's competition to build, own, and operate a crewed outpost in low Earth orbit. The company is instead putting its resources behind technologies and services that support a lasting human presence on the Moon. Aviation Week's Irene Klotz reported that Blue Origin is shelving plans for its Orbital Reef space station to focus on those lunar programs. GeekWire's Alan Boyle put it in similar terms: Blue Origin has decided not to seek further NASA funding for Orbital Reef, and the station is now on the back burner.

The company's own words are careful. In a statement emailed to Aviation Week on November 12, and also quoted by GeekWire, Blue Origin said, quote, "Blue Origin's mission is to build a road to space for the benefit of Earth so that millions of people can live and work in space. Establishing a sustained human presence on the Moon is a critical step toward that future." It went on, "As our role supporting NASA's Artemis and Moon Base missions continues to grow, we're focusing resources on capabilities that directly advance our lunar projects. By sharpening that focus, we can move faster on the technologies and infrastructure needed to make lunar permanence a reality." The statement explains why the company is concentrating on the Moon. It does not say Orbital Reef has been cancelled for good, and neither Aviation Week nor GeekWire reported a formal cancellation.

Orbital Reef has been Blue Origin's bid to help replace the International Space Station. Aviation Week notes that the station is currently slated to be decommissioned in 2030. GeekWire says NASA's next round of funding is meant to support orbital outposts that would take the place of the I.S.S. in the 2030s. For the past five years, Blue Origin has played a lead role on Orbital Reef, alongside partners that include Sierra Space and Boeing. GeekWire recalls that the project was once touted as a mixed-use business park in space, and was even slated to appear in a science-fiction thriller titled Helios. Like that delayed film, GeekWire writes, the station is now on hold.

The NASA program behind this work is called Commercial Low Earth Orbit Destinations, or C.L.D. In late 2021, according to GeekWire, Blue Origin won a 130 million dollar contract in the first phase of that program. Aviation Week reports that Blue Origin, in partnership with Sierra Space, was selected as one of three Phase 1 contractors. The other awards went to Voyager Technologies' Starlab station and to a proposal from Northrop Grumman. Those three awards followed a 2020 NASA award to Axiom Space, which proposed a station that would first attach to the I.S.S. and later separate to fly on its own. Northrop Grumman later withdrew from the competition, in October 2023, and joined the Starlab team, which Aviation Week describes as now a joint venture with Airbus.

NASA also has an unfunded Space Act Agreement with a startup called Vast, which plans to launch an early version of its station, named Haven-1, in the first quarter of 2027.

The next step is Phase 2. NASA released that solicitation on October 9. Aviation Week reports that the agency intends to award multiple firm-fixed-price, indefinite-delivery, indefinite-quantity contracts. At first, those awards would support two or more contractors through early development. A later competitive task order would then go to one or more providers for the final design, testing, evaluation, certification, and services of a commercial destination. Both Aviation Week and GeekWire reported that proposals were due on December 8, with awards expected around April 2027, or next spring. NASA had already posted Amendment 1 to the final request for proposals on November 9, and the official solicitation notice says the due date has been extended to December 14, 2026, at 1 p.m. Central Time.

Blue Origin's decision means it will not be in that bid. Aviation Week notes that, in addition to its Phase 1 C.L.D. work, Blue Origin still holds an unfunded Space Act Agreement with NASA to mature technologies and build hardware for a crewed vehicle. The company is also a primary contractor to NASA for lunar transportation services, including landing crew on the Moon.

GeekWire fills in more of that lunar picture, and it helps explain why the company says its Moon work is growing. Blue Origin is building landers to deliver cargo and crews to the lunar surface for NASA. An uncrewed Blue Moon Mark 1 lander is scheduled for launch early next year, and a crew-capable Mark 2 version is scheduled for testing in low Earth orbit next year during NASA's Artemis 3 mission. Last week, NASA awarded Blue Origin a 94 million dollar base contract, plus a 140 million dollar option, to deliver a nuclear-powered rover to the lunar south pole. The company also won a spot in NASA's broader lunar cargo delivery program. Honeybee Robotics, a Blue Origin subsidiary, will contribute to a lunar power-generating project known as miniCAST. Blue Origin is also pursuing other Moon development work, including a program called Blue Alchemist that aims to use lunar materials to manufacture solar cells and electrical wire.

Orbital Reef is not the only effort Blue Origin has paused to put the Moon first. GeekWire reports that in January, the company suspended its suborbital New Shepard program for at least two years, for similar reasons.

The field of companies still working on commercial stations is smaller, but it is not empty. GeekWire says Voyager Technologies, Airbus, and Northrop Grumman lead the Starlab consortium, which received Phase 1 funding from NASA and is expected to put in a proposal for Phase 2. Axiom Space and Vast are also developing outposts that could take the place of the I.S.S. And GeekWire notes that Gravitics, based in Marysville, Washington, is building space station hardware for Axiom and an orbital carrier for the U.S. Space Force. Neither Aviation Week nor GeekWire said what Sierra Space, Blue Origin's Orbital Reef partner, will do in the Phase 2 competition on its own.

So the picture is this. NASA is trying to line up privately owned successors to the International Space Station before that laboratory comes down around the end of the decade. Blue Origin was one of the original NASA-funded teams, with a 130 million dollar Phase 1 award and a station concept called Orbital Reef. With Phase 2 bids now due in mid-December and awards expected next spring, Blue Origin has told reporters it will not submit a proposal. The company says it wants to move faster on the lunar work already on its plate, from landers and cargo to a south-pole rover and efforts to make power systems from material on the Moon. Aviation Week describes that as shelving Orbital Reef. GeekWire describes it as putting the station on hold. What Blue Origin itself said is that it is focusing resources on capabilities that directly advance its lunar projects. That is the decision, as reported on November 13, and that is where the story stands.
