Welcome to this earnings brief for The Home Depot, ticker H-D. This episode covers the company's third quarter of fiscal twenty twenty-six. Home Depot released results before the market opened on Tuesday, November seventeenth, twenty twenty-six, and held its conference call at nine in the morning Eastern Time. It was an unusual call, because it doubled as a leadership handoff. Chair Ted Decker, back from a leave of absence, announced that Richard McPhail, the longtime chief financial officer, will become chief executive officer on November twenty-third. Ann-Marie Campbell becomes president and chief operating officer, and Jordan Broggi becomes chief financial officer. Also speaking were Billy Bastek, head of merchandising, and Mike Rowe, who runs the Pro business. This brief is based on the Motley Fool transcript of the call, the company's earnings release, and published consensus figures reported by Investing.com. I will keep the numbers short, spend most of the time on what management said and what analysts pushed on, and close with what to watch next.

Here is the quick recap. Sales were forty-three point four billion dollars, up almost five percent from a year ago, and ahead of the roughly forty-two point eight billion dollars Wall Street expected. Adjusted earnings were three dollars and eighty-six cents a share, a hair above the three eighty-four consensus. Comparable sales rose two point two percent, which McPhail called the company's best comp growth in four years, and online sales grew about thirteen percent. The weak spot was profitability: gross margin slipped from a year ago, and the company nudged its full-year margin outlook down a little even as it lifted its sales outlook. For the full year, Home Depot now expects total sales growth of about four and a half percent, comparable sales growth of about one point four percent, and adjusted earnings per share up about two percent. It also cut its organic growth expectation for SRS, the roofing and building-products distributor it bought in twenty twenty-four, from mid-single digits to low-single digits. So, a modest beat on the top and bottom line, a slightly softer margin picture, and the stock traded down about two and a half percent during the session.

Now to the part that matters most, which is how management wanted this quarter heard.

Start with the handoff, because it set the tone for the whole hour. Decker opened by thanking McPhail, Campbell, and the team for, in his words, "their outstanding leadership during my leave." He said, "I'm glad to be back," and then, "The Board and I have agreed that now is the right time to move to the next phase of our succession plan." The interim structure the company had been running, which it called the Office of the CEO, ends November twenty-third. Decker stays on as chair. He described McPhail as bringing "strategic vision, operational discipline, financial acumen and a proven track record of delivering results" over a twenty-one-year career at the company, and called Campbell, a forty-one-year veteran, someone who has driven results across stores, operations, merchandising and marketing. He closed with, "I have great confidence in Richard and Anne, our leadership team and the future of The Home Depot." Notice what was not said: Decker gave no detail about his leave, and nobody on the call was asked about it. The analysts kept it to congratulations and well wishes, and management kept it there too.

McPhail's first remarks as incoming CEO were mostly about people and continuity. He thanked Decker "for our friendship over the past twenty-one years," praised Campbell as "one of the greatest culture champions this company has ever had," and said the two of them have worked together for more than twenty years. He made a point of introducing Jordan Broggi, the new CFO, as a finance lifer who most recently ran the online business. Then he did something unusual for a CEO: he read the numbers himself and gave the financial update himself, because he was still effectively wearing both hats. That gave the call a very steady, very finance-flavored feel. The message was, nothing about the strategy changes with the new title.

His framing of the quarter was confident. "Third quarter results exceeded our expectations," he said. "We saw broad-based engagement across the business with notable strength in repair and remodel categories." And the line he kept coming back to all morning: "we are continuing to gain market share in a pressured housing market." He tied everything to the three-part strategy Decker built, which the team repeats like a mantra: core and culture, a frictionless interconnected experience, and winning with the Pro, meaning professional contractors.

Ann-Marie Campbell took the store and customer story. Her theme was execution on the floor. She talked about record on-shelf availability, job-lot quantities for contractors, and new consultative sales training for specialists who handle complex purchases like kitchens and flooring. She was most animated about express delivery, which went national about three months ago. "Delivery is available on tens of thousands of products now within two hours," she said, "with the majority of those deliveries arriving in less than one hour." She said engagement and customer satisfaction have both risen among customers who use it, and that the company has now expanded it to virtually all stores. She also stressed that stores are the backbone of online: "over half of our online orders" are fulfilled through stores. Later, in the Q&A, she put it more colorfully: "we have always said our real estate is a competitive advantage. And by gosh, it's proven to be even a better competitive advantage as we lean into complex delivery." On the Pro side, she said every Pro customer group posted positive comps, from small contractors shopping in stores to large contractors on complex projects, and she credited new tools such as an A.I. material-list builder, customer-relationship software for the Pro sales team, trade credit, and the Pro Xtra loyalty program, which she said are creating "a truly sticky relationship with our Pros."

Billy Bastek, the merchant, described what customers are actually buying, and this is the key to understanding the quarter. "Customers continue to engage in smaller repair and maintenance projects," he said. Not big remodels. Thirteen of sixteen merchandise departments were positive. Power tools were the standout, with double-digit growth, and storage, plumbing and electrical were solid. Big-ticket purchases over a thousand dollars grew, driven by power equipment and appliances. But the number of transactions actually dipped slightly, while the average ticket rose. In other words, fewer trips, bigger baskets. Bastek is proud of the battery-tool ecosystem, calling the company's nearly two thousand exclusive battery-platform products "a unique loyalty program," since once you own the battery, you keep buying the tools. He also highlighted appliance delivery, where next-day coverage on key products went from about sixty percent of the U.S. population to about eighty-five percent in just three months, and the company's own Hubspace smart-home platform, which he said is now active in millions of households. Looking to the holidays, his message was value: brands like Milwaukee, Ryobi and DEWALT in the gift center, and major appliance brands for Black Friday.

The tone overall was upbeat but measured. They emphasized share gains, steady demand, delivery speed, and the Pro. They spent very little time on margins in the prepared remarks, and they did not volunteer anything about twenty twenty-seven. That set up most of what analysts asked about.

So let's turn to the question-and-answer session, which was really a tour of investor worries.

The first worry was whether this improvement is real or just weather. Christopher Horvers of JPMorgan asked whether a warm autumn helped, given there was not much of a storm season, and whether the roughly two percent comp pace in the back half is the right starting point for twenty twenty-seven, or whether higher mortgage rates will bite later. McPhail answered the first part but sidestepped the second. "We have seen really steady demand patterns in this business," he said. On next year: "We've learned it's better to be able to manage with agility rather than to try to be great at predicting." That was a polite way of saying no twenty twenty-seven outlook today. Bastek admitted the weather helped a bit. September was much warmer, so they sold more air conditioners and fans, and a Labor Day calendar shift added a little too. But he insisted the core trend is consistent: "Maintenance and repair is certainly driving our business."

Horvers' follow-up hit the margin question directly. He pointed out that the company moved its sales outlook toward the high end while moving its operating margin toward the low end, and asked why. McPhail first praised the merchants and supply chain team for managing "years of what you call a unique cost environment." He then said the market is "much softer now than we had expected at the beginning of the year," and argued that beating the original sales plan in a weaker market proves share gains. On the margin, he said the change "is solely driven by a slight change in gross margin outlook, and that is driven simply by a change in product mix," meaning strong categories like power tools and appliances carry lower margins, plus some acquisitions, notably building up an H.V.A.C. business inside SRS. He added that the earnings outlook was also hurt slightly by a higher tax rate. His summary: "we're running the business exactly the way that we want to."

The second big worry was SRS, the roofing distributor. Steven Zaccone of Citi asked about the cut in SRS growth expectations. McPhail's answer was the most candid moment of the call. SRS did almost five billion dollars in sales in the quarter, with positive growth, he said, but "When you look at the pressure in the roofing market now, you'd have to say it's almost unprecedented. We've had two years in a row with really very little storm seasons that are really part of the normal demand pattern for roofing." He insisted the problem is "isolated" to roofing and that the other businesses inside SRS, landscape, pool, interiors and H.V.A.C., are performing well. Mike Rowe then said cross-selling between Home Depot and SRS is on track to hit its four hundred million dollar target for twenty twenty-six, and the company aims to double that in twenty twenty-seven. That doubling target is one of the few forward-looking specifics anyone offered.

Later, Seth Sigman of Barclays came back to SRS from a profit angle, asking where the cost savings from combining the businesses are showing up, and whether SRS profitability will improve. McPhail did not give a number or a timeline. He said roofing pricing is "very sharp" because of the weak demand, called it "short-term weather-driven pressure," and said the teams are building "an economic model that should see margin expansion over the long run at SRS." That is a long-run promise, not a near-term commitment, and an attentive listener would notice that the actual question about synergies was not really answered.

Zaccone also asked about fourth-quarter gross margin, which he noted was a bit weaker than the Street had expected, and whether promotions were heating up. McPhail confirmed the fourth quarter will be "a little lower than last year" on margin, again blaming product and acquisition mix. Bastek's answer here got a bit tangled. He first said, "overall, we're less promotional than we've been in prior years," and then twice said "we're certainly not less promotional." The clear point was that he does not see a promotional war and is focused on value and product authority, but the mixed wording was a reminder that management did not want to be pinned down on pricing.

The third theme was what McPhail will do differently as CEO. Michael Rehaut of Melius Research asked him directly. McPhail's answer was essentially: nothing different in direction, more intensity. "We spent a lot of time on our strategy over the last few years, and we're all bought in," he said. "We believe in it because we're winning." He described the feeling as "one of confidence that we can lean in." The one new emphasis he volunteered was artificial intelligence. "This company is going to be A.I.-enabled. We are going to lean in. We're going to be front-footed," he said, adding that A.I. is already producing efficiencies and that Home Depot's scale is "a huge advantage" there. He also mentioned a new chief technology officer, Fran Bell. His closing line: "it's full steam ahead."

Rehaut followed up on margins, asking whether the mix-driven gross margin pressure is temporary and could reverse next year. McPhail pushed back on the framing, saying the change was ten basis points from the midpoint of the original range, not twenty. He then made his philosophical case with an example from more than a decade ago, when Home Depot set out to dominate appliances, a category with below-average margins but above-average return on capital. "Accretive to sales, accretive to earnings," he said. "If there's a mix impact, we consider that somewhat secondary." On twenty twenty-seven, he again declined to guide: "we'll get to that when we get to Q4, but there's no major shift in our view on the stability of our margin profile."

Simeon Gutman of Morgan Stanley asked about two things investors care deeply about. First, how the company balances margin protection against rising costs for fuel, freight, resin and metals. Bastek said none of those cost increases were in the plan for this year, but the team managed through them, and that the priority is "protecting the project," meaning keeping the overall cost of a customer's project reasonable even if some prices go up. He acknowledged "some modest price taken across the portfolio." McPhail added, "no philosophical shifts here." Gutman's second question was the big-picture one: housing turnover is frozen, existing home sales are near a sixteen-year low, so how sustainable is this growth? McPhail was blunt. "Housing turnover hasn't moved in four years now," he said, yet when you look at the trajectory of the company's comps, "nine positive quarters in a row." "This is all coming from share gain." Then he admitted the limits: "It is pressured out there. We have the same visibility as you do." That was an honest answer, and also a reminder that management is not calling a housing recovery.

Scot Ciccarelli of Truist asked the question that probably matters most for the stock. He said trends are getting better but the strong acceleration everyone expected has not arrived, so can Home Depot grow earnings if comps stay in the low single digits? McPhail's answer was that about three percent comparable sales growth is still the "fulcrum point" for expense leverage, meaning below that pace, costs tend to grow as fast as sales. He said the company generates plenty of productivity, but "right now, given the fact our investments are working, the right equation has been to reinvest that productivity." Read plainly: at this year's pace, don't expect margin expansion, because they are choosing to keep investing. Ciccarelli also asked how much of the higher average ticket came from inflation on the same products. Bastek said about half, with the other half coming from customers trading up and from mix.

Michael Lasser of UBS asked about interest rates. He noted mortgage rates spiked during the quarter, and sales peaked in September and cooled in October, and asked if those are connected. McPhail said, "we know that a rising interest rate environment puts pressure on our customer and our business. There's no doubt about that." But he would not tie the October slowdown to rates, saying the team tries "not to look at single spikes." He did give one useful real-time data point: "the first few weeks of November are consistent with that guidance." Lasser's follow-up was sharper. He suggested Home Depot's growth is coming from lower-profit businesses, like distribution acquisitions and lower-margin categories, and asked whether investors should expect that to keep weighing on margins in twenty twenty-seven. McPhail called acquisition mix shifts "moments in time" and laid out the vision: "The goal here is to sell every product into every occasion to every customer." He conceded, "We'll probably have little fluctuations as we grow," but said he is confident in the economic model. That answer was more vision than specifics, and it did not rule out the margin drag continuing.

If you step back from the Q&A, three investor worries stood out. One, margins: the business is growing, but the products and businesses driving that growth earn less per dollar. Two, SRS and roofing: the big acquisition is fighting a historically weak roofing market, and its profit improvement is a long-run promise. Three, housing and rates: everything good this quarter came from repair and maintenance and share gains, not from a housing recovery, and management will not say when one comes. Management's answers were consistent and confident, but they dodged anything about twenty twenty-seven, gave no numbers on SRS synergies, and never addressed Decker's leave.

Now to the catalysts and what to watch next, which is where this quarter really points.

First, the leadership transition itself. McPhail officially becomes CEO on November twenty-third. Watch for whether the new team makes any strategic moves in its first few months, such as changes in capital allocation, the pace of acquisitions, or a faster push on A.I. McPhail said the strategy stays the same, but new CEOs often reshape priorities once they have the title. Also watch Jordan Broggi's first appearance as CFO; how he presents guidance will set the tone with investors.

Second, the fourth-quarter report in February, which will also likely bring the first fiscal twenty twenty-seven outlook. Every analyst who asked about next year got some version of "we'll get to that." That makes February the key event. The things to listen for are whether comps can hold near two percent, whether the company expects margins to stabilize or keep slipping from mix, and whether it plans to keep reinvesting productivity or let some flow to earnings. McPhail's three percent fulcrum comment means a low-single-digit comp outlook likely implies little or no margin expansion.

Third, holiday and early fiscal twenty twenty-seven trends. McPhail said early November was consistent with the guidance. The holiday gift center, Black Friday appliance deals, and cold-weather categories in the North will drive the quarter. Watch for any read-through from Lowe's and from monthly retail sales data.

Fourth, SRS and roofing. The company needs a more normal storm season, which is outside its control, and pricing in roofing is still tough. The cross-selling goal of doubling four hundred million dollars in twenty twenty-seven is a measurable target, so check whether management reaffirms it in February. Also watch for any number on SRS synergies, since that question went unanswered.

Fifth, interest rates and housing. Management openly admitted rising rates pressure the business. Mortgage rates, existing home sales, and home equity lending are the macro signals that would turn a steady repair-and-maintenance story into real remodel growth, or the opposite. If rates fall meaningfully, the bull case is that pent-up big projects return. If they stay high, the company is relying on share gains alone.

Sixth, costs and pricing. Fuel, freight, resin and metals costs were not in this year's plan, and the company has been taking modest price. Watch whether inflation keeps contributing about half of the ticket growth, and whether customers keep accepting it without cutting back on projects.

Finally, the operational initiatives: express delivery, next-day appliance coverage, Pro tools, and A.I. These are the investments management says are winning share. If online growth stays in double digits and Pro comps stay positive, the share-gain story holds. If those slow, the case for patience on margins gets weaker.

That's the Home Depot brief for the third quarter of fiscal twenty twenty-six. Thanks for listening.
